Profile

I turn what people write on social media into numbers, and I test whether those numbers mean what they claim. For seven years I built the systems that collect and code public posts; for the last two I have been checking what they produce: validating automated coding against human coders, separating agreement between models from validity, and asking whether a score means the same thing on every platform. My Master's project was a designed, ethics-approved quantitative study of public text.

Research interests
Credibility judgments of AI-generated information, especially when a precise confidence figure has no relation to correctness; incidental learning and informational benefits from social media and communicative AI at work; validity of automated text coding across platforms, models and time.

Analysis methods

Content analysis
Automated content analysis validated against human coding; codebook design, piloting and revision; sentiment, emotion (NRC lexicon; a 24-emotion multi-label taxonomy), need-state, toxicity, topic and intent coding; topic modelling (BERTopic) to find candidate themes; LLM-assisted annotation checked by hand; multilingual text translated before coding. Classifiers are fine-tuned encoder models (BERT, XLM-R, DistilBERT, DeBERTa-v3).
Statistics
Inter-coder reliability (Cohen's κ); agreement versus criterion validity; McNemar tests, bootstrap confidence intervals, chi-square tests; probability calibration; simulation with known ground truth; checks of coverage, denominators and whether groups can be pooled; cross-market comparison and indexing; minimum-base and small-cell rules; sensitivity analyses; cross-tabulation of respondent-level survey data.
Measurement
Generalizability theory, measurement invariance, differential item functioning and score linking, specified in a protocol and tested by simulation; not yet fitted to collected data.
Research practice
Ethics approval and GDPR-compliant handling of public text; analysis anchors fixed in advance, before any counts were computed; documented, reproducible analysis pipelines.

Research

2025
Master's project
Cross-Platform Sentiment Analysis of Public Reaction to UK Economic Policies.
  • Study design. Ethics-approved quantitative study (Portsmouth TETHIC-2025-111094) of about 279,000 public posts and comments on UK economic policy from X/Twitter, Reddit, YouTube and Quora, collected through official APIs and licensed feeds under GDPR-compliant procedures. Human-annotated validation set of 200 posts balanced across platforms (reported inter-annotator agreement Cohen's κ ≈ 0.78).
  • Classification and testing. Fine-tuned BERT classifier compared with BiLSTM by McNemar test (p < 0.001) and bootstrap intervals, alongside logistic regression and VADER baselines, with probability calibration. Chi-square tests of sentiment by policy topic; NRC emotion analysis linking anger and fear to negative sentiment in policy discussion.
  • Finding. The same policy event produced different sentiment and emotion profiles on different platforms, so a pooled index assumed, without testing, that the classifier measures the same construct everywhere.
  • Correction. Re-auditing for the manuscript, I found the headline macro-F1 of 0.878 was agreement with the lexicon-seeded training labels, not with the human set. The manuscript says so and specifies a larger stratified human criterion with a second annotator.
2026
manuscript
Ikram, A. and Haig, E. "Is the Platform Part of the Measurement? A Protocol and Simulation Study for Cross-Platform Equivalence of Machine-Labelled Policy Sentiment." Complete; not published and not submitted.
  • Treats each social platform as a survey mode and asks whether a classifier measures the same construct on each: a generalizability study against a human criterion, invariance and differential item functioning with platform as the grouping facet, and score linking. Fifteen anchor events fixed on 21 August 2026, before any window count was computed.
  • 500-replication simulation with known ground truth: an index that pools platform scores at face value misstates a known event shift by roughly an eighth and reports the wrong sign of aggregate sentiment on nearly a quarter of days; the measurement-adjusted index gets the sign right on all but about one day in sixty.
