Dear Professor Utz,

The AI system I built reported 85.8 per cent average accuracy, up from 64.7, and I believed it until I checked what it was scored against. Small business owners would act on its output, so the number had to mean what it said. When I audited it, the first three versions had been scored against 97 human-labelled posts and the last two against Claude’s labels: part of the rise was agreement with another model, not with people. A precise, climbing number had persuaded me. One anecdote proves nothing, but your study with Anderl and colleagues (2024) describes a comparable effect of another cue: people judged the same information more credible, and caught fewer of its errors, when a conversational agent presented it rather than as static text. I hold an MSc in Data Analytics (Distinction) from the University of Portsmouth and a BSc in Computer Science from FAST-NUCES, and work as a Data Engineer at M+C Saatchi Fluency and as founder and CTO of ArtemisAI. I am applying for the Researcher / PhD position in the Everyday Media Lab to test whether numeric precision is such a cue for people who did not build the system.

The measurement side of this problem first showed itself at Dubizzle Labs, where I was Senior Data Engineer from 2022 to 2024 and led three engineers collecting text in English, Arabic, Urdu and Hindi across 15 countries. People wrote differently on forums and on social platforms, so anything measured from their words depended on where it was collected. My Chevening Scholarship (2024/25) rested on a simple argument: public decisions deserve better evidence about what people think. More of that evidence now comes from AI systems, so it is only as good as a reader’s ability to tell when the system is wrong. I took that argument to the University of Portsmouth (MSc awarded December 2025; credit-weighted average 70.5, dissertation mark 78).

My dissertation was an ethics-approved study of about 279,000 posts on UK economic policy from four platforms (X, Reddit, YouTube and Quora), with a fine-tuned BERT classifier (macro-F1 0.878) tested against BiLSTM and lexicon baselines, and a 200-post human-coded validation set (Cohen’s kappa about 0.78). The same policy event looked structurally different on each platform. With my supervisor, Dr Ella Haig, I turned that finding into a methods manuscript that treats each platform as a survey mode and adapts measurement invariance and score linking to machine-labelled sentiment. In a 500-replication simulation, a naively pooled index misstates a known event shift by roughly an eighth and gives the wrong sign on nearly a quarter of days, yet it would reach a reader as one precise figure. Writing it up, I found that my own 0.878 had been scored against lexicon-seeded labels rather than the human coders, and the manuscript says so.

At Fluency, since December 2024, I have supported social listening pipelines for UK Government departments and Nike, built the ETL that cleans, translates and tags Amazon’s social data, and run a self-repairing crawler fleet that gathers owner-forum discussion for Ford. On the analysis side, where I can share how the work was done but not what it found, I classified accounts for a US women’s soccer project to separate fans from clubs, outlets and journalists, built a theme codebook from BERTopic topics I merged by hand, and LLM-tagged every post after a pilot I checked by hand and re-ran to see where the model wavered. For Abbott I coded, in LLM-assisted stages, about 170,000 multilingual posts and reviews from 12 platforms for emotions and need states; for Reckitt I set product reviews against search queries on one shared needs codebook. On a CFMOTO launch report, when the client questioned our method, we audited each brand’s mix of news and consumer posts, set a minimum consumer base, re-ran the results on narrower subsets and compared two tagging runs, and reported as directional anything the checks did not support. Treating an LLM coder as an instrument that has to be tested before its output is believed is, I think, the right preparation for studying when people believe AI output.

ArtemisAI moved the problem from whether a figure is valid to why its readers believe it. It is running in production ahead of our December launch, and each feature turns a model output into advice that a small business owner would act on without being able to check it: flop detection, which warns when a page is at risk of a weak post; crisis detection, which raises alerts from toxicity scores when a comment storm starts; and a collaboration feature that applies network theory to a graph of page affinity to suggest whom to team up with. Underneath are NLP and OCR pipelines: six fine-tuned encoder classifiers (XLM-R, DistilBERT, DeBERTa-v3, Detoxify) code sentiment, emotion, toxicity, topic, language and intent across over three million comments, alongside OCR, Whisper transcription and a CLIP-based fusion classifier for images and video. To improve the platform we drew on the research literature as well as code: Grimmer, Roberts and Stewart’s Text as Data gave us rules we are now applying to each feature, such as testing on later data held back until the end; flop detection draws on published popularity-prediction studies; and we studied the ranking code X released as open source.

We first labelled about 500 posts and comments by hand and checked Claude’s and DeepSeek’s labels against them; the classifiers now learn from two labelling tiers, Claude (about 6,000 posts) and DeepSeek (about 12,000 more), through their APIs. Even so, the headline figure I opened with ended up scored against Claude’s labels. Toxicity, on which the crisis alerts rest, showed a second problem: the two tiers used incompatible taxonomies and so agreed on none of their labels, while the toxicity model reported 96.7 per cent average confidence, a measure of certainty rather than correctness.

Your paper with Goyanes and Gil de Zúñiga (2026) describes a cue route to trust in AI news through the AI heuristic, alongside an action route through seeing AI as a social entity. Observers also read a precise figure as a sign that the speaker is confident (Jerez-Fernandez, Angulo and Oppenheimer, 2014). People already overestimate how accurate LLM answers are (Steyvers et al., 2025), and a first-person hedge such as “I’m not sure” reduces reliance on them (Kim et al., 2024). My question is whether numeric precision acts as such a cue: whether a precise confidence figure raises credibility judgements independently of accuracy, and makes errors harder to detect, more than the same confidence stated in words.

Study 1 would be a preregistered online experiment extending the accurate versus partially inaccurate paradigm of Anderl et al. (2024), crossing how confidence is stated (verbally or as a precise figure, at matched levels, against a no-confidence control) with whether the content is accurate, measuring perceived credibility and error detection. The stimuli would be LLM summaries of real social media discussion, coded against human judgement so that every statement has a known accuracy, which lets the study use errors that real systems make rather than only planted ones. Study 2 would turn to incidental learning: participants meet the same summaries in a simulated professional feed, and an unannounced later test asks what they remember people said, including the wrong figures. Anderl, Gaiser and Utz (2026) found that work-related reading and posting predicted informational benefits at the same time point but not later, suggesting predominantly short-term benefits; Study 2 asks what persists when the information comes from an AI. I would reshape both designs with you.

The answers would matter for any AI-generated summary of public opinion that professionals read at work, not only for my own product. In planning ArtemisAI’s flop warnings we argued for showing owners bands rather than single scores, but I do not know whether that helps readers catch errors or only changes how sure they feel. That is an empirical question about credibility, and it belongs in a lab like yours rather than on a product roadmap. The PhD would give my work what it has lacked, tests with people rather than only against labels, and its answers would decide how systems like mine present their output.
