The AI system I built showed its average accuracy rising from 64.7 to 85.8 per cent over five versions, and I believed it. Your study with Anderl and colleagues (2024) found that people judged the same information more credible, and caught fewer of its errors, when a conversational agent presented it rather than static text. What misled me was a different cue, a precise number that kept rising, but the effect was the same, and I had every reason to know better. ArtemisAI, the social media analytics company I founded in 2025, is where I learned to distrust my own numbers. Claude labels about 6,000 posts and DeepSeek about 12,000 more, both through their APIs, and six fine-tuned encoder classifiers learn from those labels. When I audited the rising accuracy figure, the first three versions had been scored against 97 human-labelled posts and the last two against Claude's labels, so part of the rise was agreement with another model rather than with people. On toxicity, the two labelling tiers used incompatible taxonomies and agreed on none of their labels, while the model reported 96.7 per cent average confidence. What stayed with me was my own judgement: I had built the system, and I still found its output credible on cues that told me nothing about whether it was correct.

The question is older than ArtemisAI. At Dubizzle Labs I led a team collecting text in English, Arabic, Urdu and Hindi across 15 countries, and saw that people write differently on forums and on social platforms. My MSc dissertation took that to data: an ethics-approved study of about 279,000 posts on UK economic policy from four platforms (X, Reddit, YouTube and Quora), with a fine-tuned BERT classifier (macro-F1 0.878) tested against BiLSTM and lexicon baselines, and a 200-post human-coded validation set (Cohen's kappa about 0.78). With my supervisor, Dr Ella Haig, I turned its central finding, that the same policy event looked structurally different on each platform, into a methods manuscript that treats each platform as a survey mode and adapts measurement invariance and score linking to machine-labelled sentiment. In a 500-replication simulation, a naively pooled index misstates a known event shift by roughly an eighth and gives the wrong sign on nearly a quarter of days. Writing it up, I found that my own 0.878 had been scored against lexicon-seeded labels rather than the human coders, and the manuscript says so.

Since December 2024 I have worked at M+C Saatchi Fluency, where I build data pipelines for clients including UK Government departments and, alongside the engineering, analyse what people say online for clients including Abbott, Reckitt, CFMOTO and a US women's soccer project. Treating an LLM coder as an instrument that has to be tested before its output is believed is, I think, the right preparation for studying when people believe AI output, and it is how I would build stimuli of known accuracy.

Your paper with Goyanes and Gil de Zúñiga (2026) describes a cue route to trust in AI news through the AI heuristic, alongside an action route through seeing AI as a social entity. Observers also read a precise figure as a sign that the speaker is confident (Jerez-Fernandez, Angulo and Oppenheimer, 2014). People already overestimate how accurate LLM answers are (Steyvers et al., 2025), and a first-person hedge such as “I’m not sure” reduces reliance on them (Kim et al., 2024). My question is whether numeric precision acts as such a cue: whether a precise confidence figure raises credibility judgements independently of accuracy, and makes errors harder to detect, more than the same confidence stated in words.

Study 1 would be a preregistered online experiment extending the accurate versus partially inaccurate paradigm of Anderl et al. (2024), crossing how confidence is stated (verbally or as a precise figure, at matched levels, against a no-confidence control) with whether the content is accurate. The stimuli would be LLM summaries of real social media discussion, coded against human judgement so that every statement has a known accuracy, which lets the study use errors that real systems make rather than only planted ones. Study 2 would turn to incidental learning: participants meet the same summaries in a simulated professional feed, and an unannounced later test asks what they remember people said, including the wrong figures. Anderl, Gaiser and Utz (2026) found that work-related reading and posting predicted informational benefits at the same time point but not later, suggesting predominantly short-term benefits; Study 2 asks what persists when the information comes from an AI. I would reshape both designs with you.
