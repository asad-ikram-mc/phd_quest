Dear Professor Utz,

The most consequential credibility judgment I have made about AI output was made at work, and I got it wrong. At ArtemisAI, the company I founded and where I am Chief Technology Officer, our dashboard reported an accuracy figure for the language classifiers I had built, and I trusted it as it climbed from 64.7 to 85.8 across five versions. Part of that climb was not the models improving. Up to the third version they were scored against a small set of human labels; from the fourth they were scored against labels produced by Claude, one of the two AI annotators whose output trains them, so the rising number increasingly measured agreement with another model rather than with people. The number looked authoritative because it was precise, it came from a system I understood, and it went up. I would like to spend my doctorate on exactly that kind of judgment: how people decide whether to believe information that AI has produced or filtered, and which cues they rely on when they do.

Why your lab. The Everyday Media Lab studies knowledge processes when people engage with social media or communicative AI, and encourages projects that connect the two, including the use of AI in workplace contexts. My own background sits on both sides of that connection. For seven years I have built the systems that collect what people post on social platforms, and more recently those that classify it: the social listening pipeline at M+C Saatchi Fluency that serves UK Government departments across ten platforms, and at ArtemisAI a pipeline in which AI models label social media content and smaller models learn from those labels. It runs in production ahead of our December launch. I know these systems from the inside. What I do not yet know is how people read what they produce, and that is the question your lab is built to answer.

A research question I would bring. My interest is in credibility judgments about AI-generated and AI-filtered information, in the two settings the lab names. In the workplace, professionals increasingly read AI-produced figures, summaries and confidence scores and act on them; I would ask which cues make such output believable, whether a precise number or a stated confidence raises trust independently of whether it is correct, and whether disclosing how a figure was produced changes the judgment. On social media, the same question applies to AI-generated content that people meet incidentally in their feeds. I would expect to answer these with controlled experiments that manipulate cues such as disclosure of AI origin or the presence of a confidence score, and I can add something less common to that design: large-scale social media data collected and classified at scale, to test whether effects seen in the lab hold in what people actually encounter.

My quantitative background. I hold an MSc in Data Analytics from the University of Portsmouth, awarded with Distinction in December 2025, with a project mark of 78. My dissertation was a quantitative empirical study of public reaction to UK economic policy: about 279,000 posts and comments collected from X, Reddit, YouTube and Quora under ethics approval, classified with a fine-tuned BERT model at macro-F1 0.878, validated against human coding at Cohen's kappa around 0.78, and tested with McNemar and bootstrap significance tests. Its central finding was that the same policy event produced structurally different sentiment and emotion profiles on different platforms. With my supervisor Dr Ella Haig I developed that into a methods manuscript that applies measurement invariance and score linking from psychometrics to machine-labelled text. It is complete but not yet submitted or published.
