With my supervisor, Dr Ella Haig, I wrote a methods manuscript that treats each platform as a survey mode and sets out a protocol adapting measurement invariance, differential item functioning and score linking to machine-labelled sentiment; in a 500-replication simulation, a naively pooled index gave the wrong sign on nearly a quarter of days. Anderl et al. (2024) found that the same information was judged more credible when a conversational agent presented it than as static encyclopedia text, and that readers were worse at spotting its inaccuracies. My dashboard was a workplace version: a presentation cue carrying credibility the content had not earned. Utz, Goyanes and Gil de Zúñiga (2026) describe a cue route to trust in AI news through the AI heuristic, alongside an action route through seeing AI as a social entity. Observers read a precise figure as a sign that the speaker is confident (Jerez-Fernandez, Angulo and Oppenheimer, 2014), and I would test whether a precise figure or a stated confidence acts as such a cue for AI output; communicative AI at work produces both routinely.

My question is whether numeric precision and stated confidence in LLM answers and summaries raise credibility independently of accuracy and make errors harder to detect. I expect both. I would extend the accurate versus partially inaccurate paradigm of Anderl et al. (2024) to knowledge workers and to errors that real systems make, measuring error detection and retention rather than trust alone. I can build those stimuli: LLM summaries of real social media discussion, coded against human judgement, so every statement has a known accuracy. Natural errors may be harder to spot than planted ones, which bears on how far laboratory estimates generalise. Study 1 would be a preregistered online experiment crossing how confidence is stated (verbally or as a precise figure, at matched levels, against a no-confidence control) with whether the content is accurate, measuring credibility judgements and error detection. Study 2 would test incidental learning: participants meet the same summaries in a simulated professional feed, and an unannounced later test asks what they believe people said, including the wrong figures, and whether precision made those stick. Anderl, Gaiser and Utz (2026) found informational benefits of work-related social media use in the moment but no meaningful lagged effects; Study 2 asks what persists when the information comes from an AI.
