## LINE: kim-andersen

### Andersen's own methods are survey-panel, experimental and psychometric, not computational — he collaborates for the computational work, and that collaborator is Rasmus Schmøkel.
Across his verified record his workhorse designs are multi-wave panel surveys, latent class/transition models, choice experiments and cultivation analysis: "Selective News Avoidance: Consistency and Temporality" (Andersen, Shehata, Skovsgaard, Strömbäck, Communication Research, online 2024 / issue 53(3) 2026, doi 10.1177/00936502231221689) uses a five-wave Swedish panel; "Maintenance and Reformation of News Repertoires: A Latent Transition Analysis" (Andersen, Johansson, Johansson, Shehata, Journalism & Mass Communication Quarterly, 2021, doi 10.1177/10776990211019750) uses a four-wave Swedish panel and LTA to derive four repertoire classes (Public service-oriented traditionalists, Minimalists, Engaged pluralists, Quality-oriented explorers); "The Scary World Syndrome" (Andersen, Djerf-Pierre, Shehata, Mass Communication and Society, 2024, doi 10.1080/15205436.2023.2297829) pairs a content analysis with a three-wave panel. He has one sole-authored field experiment, "Realizing Good Intentions? A Field Experiment of Slow News Consumption and News Fatigue" (Journalism Practice, 2020, doi 10.1080/17512786.2020.1818609). Where machine classification enters, a computational co-author appears: Rasmus W. Schmøkel, who defended his SDU PhD in August 2025 ("The Impact of Algorithms on News Consumption", doi 10.21996/655ab7dc-49d2-4d2a-b1fa-79c5d35bc937) and whose own prior work is computer vision — "FBAdLibrarian and Pykognition: open science tools for the collection and emotion detection of images in Facebook political ads with computer vision" (Schmøkel & Bossetta, Journal of Information Technology & Politics, 2021). Practical reading: Asad is not competing with Andersen on method, he is supplying a capability Andersen currently buys in. The proposal should be legible to a panel-survey psychometrician, not written as an ML paper.
*Use in: both*

### The single most important document to read before writing is his group's unpublished working paper — it reveals that their current automated content analysis is GPT-4 prompting validated on 300 items, and that they measure quality with Krippendorff's alpha, not F1.
"The Paradox of Negative News: Disentangling the Dual Role of Negativity Bias in News Selection and Avoidance", Lucía Cores-Sarría (Universidad Carlos III de Madrid), Kim Andersen, Rasmus Schmøkel and Morten Skovsgaard (SDU), working paper dated 28 November 2025, SocArXiv doi 10.31235/osf.io/qft3e_v1. Verbatim from its Measures section: "OpenAI's API (ChatGPT-4, May 2025) was used to automatically code the valence of the unique news posts in the raw dataset (n = 11,738) into three categories: positive, neutral, and negative." Validation: "a random subsample of 300 news posts was independently coded by two human coders (two of the authors). Krippendorff's alpha (ordinal) indicated high agreement between the two human coders (α = 0.85), and substantial agreement between each human coder and ChatGPT (α = 0.80 and α = 0.79, respectively)." Corpus: Danish news publisher Facebook Pages harvested via Meta's now-deprecated CrowdTangle API; valence distribution 72.5% negative, 20.2% positive, 7.2% neutral in the raw set. Design: a self-paced Facebook-lookalike feed, Denmark, fieldwork 27 June to 12 November 2023, 5,172 completed the pre-survey, 2,495 completed the first platform session (55.97% dropout), 81,765 post-level observations reduced by outlier cleaning to 21,633 observations across 668 subjects. Analysis in R with lme4/lmerTest, cross-classified multilevel models with random intercepts for subject and post. Finding: negativity bias is real but short-lived — it fades within seconds, while positive news better resists the natural decline in engagement. This is the exact seam for Asad. Their negativity instrument is a single closed-vendor prompt, validated once, on 300 items, by two of the authors, against no held-out benchmark, on the news half of the corpus only ("Non-news, entertainment posts, which constituted 50% of the dataset, were not coded for valence"). Asad's Portsmouth dissertation (macro-F1 0.878, human kappa ~0.78) and his ArtemisAI encoder stack are directly comparable capabilities, and his Haig manuscript is a ready-made argument for why a single unvalidated instrument applied across outlets and platforms will not carry a cross-platform comparison.
*Use in: research proposal*

### He has just published a paper reporting the opposite of the negativity bias his own project is named after. A proposal that treats negativity bias as settled fact will read as under-informed.
"Challenging the Spiral of Negativity: A Longitudinal Choice Experiment on the Preference for Valenced News and Reinforcement of Societal Beliefs", Kim Andersen and Adam Shehata, The International Journal of Press/Politics, published 16 September 2025, doi 10.1177/19401612251356664. From the abstract: it is a "preregistered longitudinal choice experiment" on positive and negative emphasis framing of news about Swedish schools, and "Contrary to the hypothesis of a general negativity bias in news selection, the results indicate a higher preference for positive news"; confirmation bias was supported, and "exposure to both positive and negative news led to more positive beliefs over time, suggesting that information effects were more influential than framing effects". The paper closes by saying the findings "challenge central claims within the literature on biases in news selection". Combined with the Paradox working paper's time-decaying negativity bias, the pattern is clear: Andersen's contribution to this literature is complicating a claim everyone else asserts. He preregisters. A proposal should treat the amount and character of negativity as an open empirical and measurement question, not as a premise.
*Use in: research proposal*

### His newest paper is a measurement paper: different operationalisations of news avoidance produce different substantive conclusions. This is the same claim as Asad's unpublished manuscript with Dr Haig, in Andersen's own field.
"Is News Avoidance a Democratic Problem or a Healthy Audience Behavior?" — Andersen, Hopmann, Langmann, Schemer and Skovsgaard, Mass Communication and Society, 2026 (listed on his Google Scholar as his most recent output). Its earlier conference version, listed on the THREATPIE project outputs page, carries the fuller title: "Democratic Problem or Healthy Audience Practice? How Different Operationalization of News Avoidance Relate to Political Knowledge, Efficacy, and Life Satisfaction", NORFACE GOVERNANCE Final Conference 2024, Vienna. This is the strongest single point of contact in his whole record with what Asad actually has: a manuscript that treats each platform as a survey mode and applies generalizability theory, measurement invariance, DIF and score linking to machine-labelled sentiment, with a 500-replication simulation showing a pooled cross-platform index reports the wrong sign of aggregate sentiment on roughly 25% of days. Andersen is already persuaded, in the survey world, that operationalisation drives conclusions. The proposal's job is to carry that argument into SP1's machine-coded, multi-source setting — where news outlet text, platform posts and donated feed items are three different measurement modes that no one has shown to be comparable.
*Use in: both*

### He has a public track record of attacking sloppy convention in Danish journalism research by name. He is a measurement sceptic before he is a data enthusiast.
"Debat: En problematisk populær metode i journalistikforskningen", Kim Andersen and Erik Gahner Larsen, Journalisten.dk, 3 November 2017. They attack the Danish convention of analysing "uge 46" (week 46) as a stand-in for general media coverage: the week was picked for one 1999 study and then applied uncritically, and major events keep landing in it (the 2007 parliamentary election, the 2015 Paris attacks), so, in their words, "Man kan ikke uden videre bruge uge 46 til at sige noget om den generelle mediedækning" — one cannot simply use week 46 to say something about general media coverage. Their fix is random-day sampling across the target period, on the logic of opinion polling. He co-wrote a companion piece the same year questioning the widely repeated claim that 92% of Danish news stories are shared on Facebook. This tells you what impresses him: sampling frames that are defensible, and someone willing to say a popular number is wrong. It also tells you what will irritate him — a proposal that promises volume ("68 collectors", "279,000 posts") without stating what population the sample represents and what the sampling frame is.
*Use in: both*

### SDU already has the experimental-platform and behavioural-tracking infrastructure. The gap Asad fills is data collection and measurement validation, not platform building — and he should be careful not to claim Schmøkel's territory.
The Digital Democracy Centre at SDU runs "The Experimental Social Media Platform (ESMP), SDU Social", led by Rasmus W. Schmøkel, Claes de Vreese and Narongrit Umwerawattana: a research platform that varies feed composition, interface and algorithmic logic while tracking behaviour, integrating survey data and behavioural tracking. It is the platform used in the Paradox of Negative News study. DDC also runs "Countering News Avoidance with Personalized News Formats (NAPNF)". Separately, SDU Metaverse Lab (contact Anders Drachen) is a partner in the Smart Data Donation Service consortium, which builds tools letting people exercise data portability rights to donate data from companies such as Google and TikTok — so data donation know-how exists at SDU but in a different building from the Centre for Journalism, with no visible journalism link. Kim Andersen is listed as a DDC member (Associate Professor, Centre for Journalism, and affiliated researcher at the Department of Journalism, Media and Communication, University of Gothenburg; kand@journalism.sdu.dk). Implication for the proposal: do not offer to build a feed simulator or a recommender testbed — that is Schmøkel's PhD, already delivered. Offer the acquisition-and-measurement layer: DDP (data download package) parsing across platforms, a repair layer for schema drift when Meta and TikTok change their export formats, a walk-up hub workflow that works in a shopping centre or library with a stranger's phone, and a validated negativity instrument with reported reliability per source.
*Use in: research proposal*

### He co-authored the field's agenda-setting review and named its blind spots himself. Two of the three named gaps are things an SP1 content-and-feeds PhD can actually address.
"Introduction: What We (Don't) Know About News Avoidance", Kim Andersen, Benjamin Toff and Brita Ytre-Arne, Journalism Studies 25(12), 2024, pp. 1367-1384, doi 10.1080/1461670X.2024.2393131 — a systematic review of N = 116 studies, and by Google Scholar's count already his second-most-cited recent piece (55 citations). Its stated conclusions: news avoidance is multidimensional with behavioural and expressive components; research is mostly quantitative and mostly Western; "we know relatively much about causes for news avoidance and some of its consequences, but less about counterstrategies"; and "while much of our knowledge is centered around audiences, we know little about the roles of other actors, such as the news media, journalists, and intermediaries". It ends by stressing "the need to carefully consider normative assumptions when positioning news avoidance as a problem". The intermediaries gap is the opening: an SP1 design that measures negativity at three levels — what outlets publish, what platforms surface, what individual feeds actually contain — is a direct measurement of the intermediary's contribution, which he has said in print the field does not have. Citing this paper back to him and taking up the intermediary gap explicitly is the highest-value single citation available.
*Use in: research proposal*

*Use in: motivated application*

### The project's normative destination is public advice, and Andersen has already published his draft of that advice in a national newspaper. A proposal that ends in a measurement instrument with no route to a recommendation will miss what he is trying to buy.
Andersen's op-ed "Det er på tide med nogle klare nyhedsråd, der kan hjælpe forbrugerne med at skabe og sikre en sund nyhedsdiæt" ran in Politiken on 30 March 2025. It gives five pieces of news advice, explicitly on a dietary analogy: build awareness of how news affects you; set boundaries on when and how much you consume, since "At undgå nyheder i et moderat omfang er intet problem" (avoiding news in moderation is no problem at all); choose sources carefully, and specifically "Sociale platforme bør heller ikke være din primære nyhedskilde" because algorithms prioritise what generates likes, shares and comments, so "negative og dramatiske historier får ekstra meget opmærksomhed"; prioritise depth over speed; and seek out positive and constructive news. Note the productive tension for a proposal: this op-ed asserts that platform algorithms amplify negativity, while his own experimental evidence in the Paradox paper shows the negativity advantage in engagement decays within seconds. Whether donated feeds actually contain more negativity than the outlets that fed them is an open, testable question, and it is precisely the question data donation hubs can answer that neither his surveys nor his simulated feed can. A proposal that frames itself as testing his own public claim, with a measurement design good enough for the answer to be trusted, hits the intellectual and the funder-facing target at once.
*Use in: research proposal*

### What would impress him, and what would make him sceptical — read off the record rather than guessed.
Impressed by: a defensible sampling frame and an explicit population, stated before any model is mentioned (the week 46 critique); preregistration and a willingness to report a result that contradicts the expected direction (the IJPP 2025 paper); reliability reported the way communication research reports it — Krippendorff's alpha or Cohen's kappa against human coders on a documented subsample, not only a macro-F1 (his own GPT-4 validation and Asad's kappa ~0.78 are directly commensurable, so lead with kappa and mention F1 second); named handling of ethics, consent and GDPR, since his own methods sections say data were stored on university servers under GDPR-compliant procedures with ethics board approval and prior informed consent, and hubs collecting strangers' social media exports in public spaces make this the project's biggest live risk; and evidence of the boring durability that makes an infrastructure work — a repair layer that survives a platform changing its export format, which is exactly what Asad has been paid to build for seven years and which no academic on the team has. Sceptical of: any claim that a large-N social media corpus speaks for a population; sentiment or emotion classifiers ported into Danish without in-language validation, since the entire evidence base here is Danish-language, his existing corpus is Danish Facebook posts, and Asad's own models were built and validated on English; a proposal that reads as an engineering plan with theory bolted on, since he is a theory-and-design person publishing in Communication Research, Journal of Communication and IJPP; and overclaiming on the CV — he is a scholar who publicly corrected a widely cited Danish statistic, so an unpublished manuscript should be described as unpublished, and things Asad has not done (fine-tuned generative LLMs, human-subjects studies, IRB approval) should not be implied.
*Use in: both*

## LINE: the-team

### The phrase "the amount and character of negativity" in the advert is a direct lift from a canonical conceptual paper Hopmann's own circle wrote. Treating negativity as one sentiment score would read as illiterate to this panel.
Journalism 13(2), 2012 was a guest-edited special issue by Esser, Strömbäck & de Vreese titled "Studying Political News: Towards a Standardization of Core Concepts." It contains BOTH Lengauer, Esser & Berganza, "Negativity in political news: A review of concepts, operationalizations and key findings," Journalism 13(2): 179-202 (doi 10.1177/1464884911427800) AND Hopmann, Van Aelst & Legnante, "Political balance in the news: A review of concepts, operationalizations and key findings," Journalism 13(2) (doi 10.1177/1464884911427804). Lengauer et al. distinguish three axes: (1) negativity vs confrontation, (2) frame-related vs individual actor-related negativity, (3) non-directional vs directional negativity, and supply a matrix-based classification of intensity and multi-dimensionality. Hopmann literally co-wrote the sibling review in the same issue, on balance. So "amount" = intensity/prevalence and "character" = which cell of that matrix. A proposal that operationalises negativity as a single continuous polarity score per document collapses all three axes at once.

## LINE: negativity-literature

### Even the canonical negativity-bias study measures negativity by hand, second by second, and its own effect does not replicate across countries.
Soroka, Fournier & Nir (2019, PNAS 116(38):18888-18892) ran 1,156 participants in 17 countries (incl. Denmark) through 7 randomly ordered BBC World News stories, recording skin conductance (nSCL) and heart-rate variability (RMSSD). Negativity was, in their words, "an interval-level measure based on the average of second-by-second coding by expert coders" -- no dictionary, no classifier. The nSCL effect was worth about 65% of the observed SD; the RMSSD shift about 10% of SD, and RMSSD reached significance in only 5 of the 17 countries. Use this two ways: (a) it is the load-bearing citation for negativity bias and Asad must cite it, (b) it shows the field's gold standard for measuring negativity is expensive human coding that cannot scale to donated feeds, which is the opening SP1 needs.
*Use in: research proposal*

### The field's own standard-setting review says negativity has never been a single concept, and its five coding variables are still what current classifiers are built on.
Lengauer, Esser & Berganza, "Negativity in political news: A review of concepts, operationalizations and key findings", Journalism 13(2):179-202 (2012; online-first 2011). They state negativity "has not reached the status of a homogenous, overarching theoretical concept" and draw three distinctions: negativity vs confrontation; frame-related vs individual-actor-related; non-directional vs directional. The DOCA content-analysis variable database codifies their five -1/0/+1 coding variables: negative tone toward political actors; overall story tonality (failure/fiasco/crisis vs success/solutions); pessimistic outlook; conflict-centredness; incapability and misconduct. This is still live: Talaga et al. (2025) built their transformer annotation guidelines directly on Lengauer's exogenous ('mere dissemination of negative news') vs endogenous ('negativity imposed on news by journalists through their usage of language') split, p. 181.
*Use in: both*

### The concrete instruments in use are a small, nameable set -- and the choice is almost never justified.
Dictionary/lexicon tier: Lexicoder Sentiment Dictionary (Young & Soroka, Political Communication 29(2):205-231, 2012) -- 4,576 positive and negative words and phrases drawn from Roget's Thesaurus, the General Inquirer and the Regressive Imagery Dictionary, validated against 900 news stories coded by three humans; LIWC (620 positive, 744 negative words) used by Robertson et al. 2023; VADER used by Watson et al. 2024; NRC, SentiStrength, DANEW, Pattern, Polyglot. Human tier: Lengauer's five-variable codebook. Supervised tier: XLM-RoBERTa-Large binary classifiers (Talaga et al. 2025). Discrete-emotion tier: Soroka, Young & Balmas, "Bad News or Mad News? Sentiment Scoring of Negativity, Fear, and Anger in News Content", ANNALS 659:108-121 (2015) -- the paper that separates negativity from fear and anger, which maps onto Asad's NRC emotion work in the MSc dissertation.
*Use in: research proposal*

### THE KEY PAPER: seven sentiment instruments run on the same news corpus agree with human coders at Krippendorff's alpha of at most .35, and with each other at an average r of .33.
Boukes, van de Velde, Araujo & Vliegenthart, "What's the Tone? Easy Doesn't Do It", Communication Methods and Measures 14(2):83-104 (2020), open access. 4,640 manually coded Dutch economic headlines plus full texts (human intercoder Krippendorff alpha .80 headlines, .69 full text). Best agreement with humans: headlines, combined approach alpha .298, Polyglot .250, LIWC .226, SentiStrength .179, Pattern .169; full texts, LIWC .349, Combined .315, Polyglot .258, Pattern .087. Krippendorff's own threshold is .667 for tentative conclusions. Tool-to-tool agreement is worse: SentiStrength vs Pattern alpha .09 (headline) and .07 (full text). Two further points Asad can build on: aggregating to weekly level lifts correlations to roughly .60, and they cite van der Wees et al. (2015) that GENRE affects algorithm accuracy more than TOPIC -- because tools like SentiStrength and Pattern were built on informal user-generated text and are being applied to journalistic prose. Their conclusion: "off-the-shelf sentiment analysis tools are mostly unreliable and unsuitable for research purposes" and manual validation "for the specific language, domain, and genre" is always warranted.
*Use in: both*

### There is now a published case where the substantive conclusion about negativity flips when you swap the instrument -- and it is on the most-cited negativity paper of the decade.
Robertson, Proellochs, Schwarzenegger, Paernamets, Van Bavel & Feuerriegel, "Negativity drives online news consumption", Nature Human Behaviour (2023), 105,000 Upworthy headline variants, ~5.7m clicks over ~370m impressions, N=22,743 in RCTs. Scored with LIWC: each additional negative word raises CTR by 2.3%, each positive word lowers it by 1.0%; discrete emotions gave sadness beta=.006 (p<.001), joy beta=-.009, fear beta=-.007, anger n.s. (p=.666). They ran NRC and SentiStrength as robustness checks and found "coefficient estimates were in good agreement". Then Reiss & Roggenkamp, "A Comment on 'Negativity Drives Online News Consumption'", I4R Discussion Paper 199 (2025): they reproduce both claims with lexical sentiment, but when positivity is measured semantically with large language models instead of by word lists, positive words no longer reduce click rate. The negativity claim survives; the positivity claim was an artefact of the instrument. Note this is instrument-vs-instrument on ONE corpus, not the same instrument across outlets -- which is exactly the gap Asad occupies.
*Use in: both*

### Asad's specific claim -- same instrument, different platform, different meaning -- has been OBSERVED but never framed as a measurement problem or tested properly. This is the most important finding for his novelty claim.
Pellert, Metzler, Matzenberger & Garcia, "Validating daily social media macroscopes of emotions", Scientific Reports 12:11236 (2022). They validated aggregated text sentiment against daily self-reported mood from users of the Austrian newspaper Der Standard, then replicated on Twitter. The LIWC negative-affect component correlates with self-reported positive mood at rho = 0.03 [-0.42, 0.46] on the Der Standard forum but -0.74 [-0.89, -0.43] on Twitter. The same dictionary, the same language, the same construct, two platforms, and one of the two readings is noise. They call this out explicitly -- it "point[s] to a potentially missing methodological link that connects which kind of sentiment captures which emotions or sentiments on social media text" -- and note that "asymmetry in measurement error when detecting positive versus negative sentiment ... has been shown for English to be potentially different across various social media". They explain it post hoc by Austrian dialect prevalence on the forum. There is no invariance test, no DIF, no linking. That is the hole Asad's manuscript with Dr Haig is shaped to fill.
*Use in: both*

### Danish evidence, on a public benchmark, that the instrument-format interaction is large -- AFINN loses 20 accuracy points moving from parliamentary text to Twitter while a transformer loses six.
The DaNLP benchmark (Alexandra Institute) scores Danish sentiment tools on three test sets. AFINN: 0.68 Europarl, 0.66 LCC Sentiment, 0.48 Twitter. Sentida: 0.67 / 0.58 / 0.44. BERT Tone: 0.79 / 0.74 / 0.73. SpaCy sentiment: 0.74 / 0.66 / 0.66. Senda (Ekstra Bladet): 0.75 / 0.68 / 0.76. Sentida is Lauridsen, Aarup Dalsgaard & Svendsen, "SENTIDA: A New Tool for Sentiment Analysis in Danish", Journal of Language Works 4(1) (2019), validated on Trustpilot reviews at >80% accuracy. This is the single most usable fact in the whole line: SP1 compares news outlets (formal prose) against donated social feeds (informal), and on Danish data the lexicon tools collapse on exactly that transition while the neural ones hold. Asad can propose the invariance test as the thing that decides which instrument SP1 should trust, with numbers already on the table.
*Use in: research proposal*

### Rank order of sentiment methods is not stable across corpora at all -- 24 methods, 18 datasets, no winner.
Ribeiro, Araujo, Goncalves, Goncalves & Benevenuto, "SentiBench", EPJ Data Science 5:23 (2016). 24 sentiment methods across 18 labelled datasets spanning tweets, movie and product reviews, news comments (BBC, Digg, NYT), YouTube and TED comments, and forum posts. "There is no single method that always achieves the best prediction performance for all different datasets"; VADER ranks 1st on one dataset and 10th-13th on others; Friedman tests confirm ranking differences at p<.05. Their line is quotable: "the same social media text can be interpreted very differently depending on the choice of a sentiment method."
*Use in: both*

### A 2025 six-country study finds negative news is RARE and gets LESS engagement, directly contradicting Robertson and Watson -- and blames outlet heterogeneity, plus its own instrument disagrees with itself across formats one time in five.
Talaga, Batorski & Wojcieszak, arXiv:2507.19300 (July 2025). 6,081,134 Facebook posts from 97 outlets in the US, UK, Ireland, Poland, France and Spain, Jan 2020 - Apr 2024. Two XLM-RoBERTa-Large classifiers; negativity operationalised from Lengauer et al. Findings: only ~12.6% of posts negative; political posts no more negative than non-political; US political posts ~40% lower odds of negativity than other countries; negative posts get ~15% fewer likes and ~13% fewer comments; only 10.2-13.1% of total engagement attaches to negative posts. Two measurement details Asad should lift. (1) Negativity classifier F1 varies by country: 0.95 US, 0.92 Ireland, 0.91 France, 0.90 Poland, 0.89 Spain, 0.88 UK -- differential performance by language, reported but not interrogated. (2) Appendix B.3: labels from post text vs labels from full article text agree in only 77.9% [72.3-83.5] of cases for negativity. The same classifier, the same story, two formats, disagreeing on roughly one story in five. Their Appendix D attributes the clash with prior work to "high outlet-level heterogeneity of negativity effects" -- effects, note, not measurement.
*Use in: both*

### Negativity measured on articles vs on the posts about them differs sharply -- and the instrument used is one built for social media and applied to journalism.
Watson, van der Linden, Watson & Stillwell, "Negative online news articles are shared more to social media", Scientific Reports (2024), DOI 10.1038/s41598-024-71263-z. VADER on 95,282 articles from the Daily Mail, Guardian, New York Times and New York Post (2019-2021), against 579,182,075 Facebook and Twitter posts. 29-42% of articles are negative depending on outlet, but "news articles are consistently less negative than the tweets that concern them, with all tweet samples being predominantly negative". Negative articles 1.91x more likely to be shared; +30-150% Facebook shares depending on publication; +26-36% on Twitter; effect larger on Facebook than Twitter and larger for right-leaning than left-leaning papers. Asad's line: that article/post negativity gap is reported as a substantive finding, but VADER was constructed for social-media text, so a gap of exactly that direction is what a genre-mismatched instrument would produce anyway. The study cannot tell the two apart. Neither can SP1 unless someone tests it.
*Use in: both*

### Method of data collection changes the answer about valence preference, in a peer-reviewed election study.
Maier, Maier, Gorski & Schmidt, "Beyond a Negativity Bias: Explaining the Consumption of Positive and Negative Political Information Using WebTracking and Experience Sampling Data", Journalism & Mass Communication Quarterly (2025), DOI 10.1177/10776990251359658. 2021 German federal election, desktop web tracking plus mobile experience sampling (MILLA). "Positivity and negativity are equally relevant but vary across channels and methods of data collection." Different predictors emerge depending on the instrument: gender and conflict approach in tracking data; perceived duty, extreme ideology and conflict approach in the self-report data. Reported figures elsewhere in the literature summary: tracking showed news sites carrying more positive (62%) than negative (34%) information, while self-report put negativity higher for print (65% negative) and internet (72% negative). This is the mode-effect argument Asad already knows how to formalise.
*Use in: both*

### News avoidance research has the same measurement disease, and Toff's group says so out loud -- which gives Asad a way to connect SP1 to SP2 and SP3.
Palmer, Toff & Nielsen, "Examining Assumptions Around How News Avoidance Gets Defined", Journalism Studies 24(6):697-714 (2023): "News avoidance research has been hampered by confusion about how to define and operationalize the concept." Using Reuters Institute DNR data from 46 markets plus 108 interviews with low-news consumers in the UK, US and Spain, they show most people who say they selectively avoid news consume almost as much news as those who do not, and that sorting avoiders by stated motivation misdescribes how habits actually form. The typology Asad must cite alongside it is Skovsgaard & Andersen, "Conceptualizing News Avoidance", Journalism Studies 21(4):459-476 (2020) -- intentional (dislike of negativity, distrust, feeling overwhelmed) vs unintentional (changed opportunity structures) -- co-authored by the project lead. Qualitative field: Villi, Aharoni, Tenenboim-Weinblatt, Boczkowski, Hayashi, Mitchelstein, Tanaka & Kligler-Vilenchik, "Taking a Break from News", Digital Journalism 10(1):148-164 (2021), 488 interviews across Argentina, Finland, Israel, Japan and the US, distinguishing cognitive drivers (country-specific) from emotional drivers (shared across contexts). Also Toff & Nielsen, "'I Just Google It': Folk Theories of Distributed Discovery", Journal of Communication 68(3):636-657 (2018); Toff & Palmer on the gender gap, Journalism Studies 20(11):1563-1579 (2018).
*Use in: both*

### Denmark's news avoidance jumped 7 points this year -- the joint-largest rise in the survey. That is the sentence to open a Danish proposal with.
Reuters Institute Digital News Report 2026: 42% across markets say they sometimes or often avoid the news, flat year on year but up from 29% in 2017; roughly 97,520 respondents across 48 markets, fieldwork January-February 2026; item wording "Do you find yourself actively trying to avoid news these days?". Denmark is at 34%, up 7 points year on year, with Norway up 9 -- the two biggest movers. Denmark's trust in news is 55% against a 37% global average, down 1 point from a 2021 peak of 59%; most trusted are public service and legacy outlets, least trusted the two national tabloids. Across markets, the share describing themselves as extremely or very interested in news has fallen an average of 13 points since 2021. Note the 2025 report gave 40% for 2025 and the 2026 report gives 42% for the same year, so the series has been restated -- quote the 2026 report only.
*Use in: both*

### The project lead's own newest paper finds a preference for POSITIVE news. Asad must not write a proposal that assumes negativity bias is settled.
Andersen, K. & Shehata, A., "Challenging the Spiral of Negativity: A Longitudinal Choice Experiment on the Preference for Valenced News and Reinforcement of Societal Beliefs", The International Journal of Press/Politics, published online 16 September 2025, DOI 10.1177/19401612251356664. Preregistered longitudinal choice experiment on positive vs negative emphasis framing of news about Swedish schools. "Contrary to the hypothesis of a general negativity bias in news selection, the results indicate a higher preference for positive news." Confirmation bias was supported -- people with more negative beliefs chose more negative news -- and exposure to both positive and negative news produced more positive beliefs over time, so information effects beat framing effects. They conclude these findings "challenge central claims within the literature on biases in news selection". Related: Andersen, Djerf-Pierre & Shehata, "The Scary World Syndrome: News Orientations, Negativity Bias, and the Cultivation of Anxiety", Mass Communication and Society (2024), DOI 10.1080/15205436.2023.2297829; and Andersen, Shehata, Skovsgaard & Stroembaeck, "Selective News Avoidance: Consistency and Temporality", Communication Research (2024), DOI 10.1177/00936502231221689, a five-wave Swedish panel showing selective avoidance varies both between persons (consistency) and within persons (temporality). Strategic read: Andersen keeps finding that the standard claims do not hold when you measure carefully. A proposal whose contribution is 'measure it properly before you claim it' is aimed straight at him.
*Use in: motivated application*

### Doomscrolling is measured entirely by self-report and has never been linked to the measured negativity of the feed a person actually saw. Data donation closes that loop.
Sharma, Lee & Johnson, "The dark at the end of the tunnel: Doomscrolling on social media newsfeeds", Technology, Mind, and Behavior 3(1):144-156 (2022), DOI 10.1037/tmb0000059. A 15-item unidimensional self-report scale (DS-15) built from focus groups and an expert panel, tested on an OECD panel of N=401, 7-point Likert. Downstream work associates doomscrolling with anxiety, depression, distress and reduced well-being. Every one of those studies asks people how much bad news they think they consumed. The HEALTHY NEWS data donation hubs would, for the first time, put a measured negativity score on the actual feed alongside the self-report -- which is a clean, concrete SP1-to-SP3 bridge Asad can offer, and it is the kind of validity claim only someone who can build the pipeline can make.
*Use in: research proposal*

### Constructive journalism is the field's proposed remedy, and the strongest experimental evidence is by an SDU alumnus.
Overgaard, C.S.B., "Mitigating the consequences of negative news: How constructive journalism enhances self-efficacy and news credibility", Journalism (2023), DOI 10.1177/14648849211062738, n=270: constructive social-media posts, compared with negative posts, produced higher positive affect, higher self-efficacy and higher perceived news credibility. Overgaard holds an MSc from the University of Southern Denmark and is now at the Center for Media Engagement. Denmark is the home of the Constructive Institute (Aarhus), which makes constructive journalism the obvious policy end-point a Danish reviewer will expect a negativity proposal to gesture at. The measurement angle Asad can add: 'constructive' is currently operationalised as a manipulated stimulus in experiments, not as a measurable property of real coverage, so nobody can yet say how much constructive journalism is actually in a Danish news diet.

## LINE: data-donation

### The canonical citation for the method is Boeschoten, Ausloos, Möller, Araujo and Oberski's Total Error Framework for data donation — cite it and name its ten error sources to show you know the method's own quality vocabulary.
arXiv:2011.09851 (13 Nov 2020), published as "A framework for privacy preserving digital trace data collection through data donation", Computational Communication Research 4(2), 2022. It adapts Groves and Lyberg's Total Survey Error to DDPs and splits errors into a measurement side — construct (in)validity, measurement error, extraction error, algorithmic error, integration error — and a representation side — coverage error, sampling error, nonresponse error, compliance error, consent error. Two of these are unique to donation. Compliance error: "attrition is likely to occur due to the fact that respondents are not willing or able to invest the time and effort in this procedure". Consent error: the participant reviews the extracted CSV and refuses or partly refuses, which the authors warn is likely missing-not-at-random and "could be related to topics of interest measured within the DDPs" — i.e. if negativity is the construct, people who saw the most negative feed may be exactly the ones who withhold. Their proposed mitigation is linking DDPs to a parallel survey so missingness can be treated as MAR. Critically for Asad: "algorithmic error is the typical classification or prediction error" in supervised modelling, and they explicitly say the ground truth used by supervised models is itself "an error-prone and unobserved variable" — an accurate estimate of the algorithmic error rate makes it possible to "handle downstream decisions more adequately, by using standard measurement error models".
*Use in: both — the theoretical/methodological anchor of the proposal, and the hook for Asad's measurement-error expertise*

### The single strongest bridge from Asad's dissertation to SP1 is the algorithmic-error node of the Total Error Framework: Boeschoten et al. name it but leave it as prediction error on an error-prone ground truth, and nobody has propagated classifier error into donated-feed estimates.
The framework treats the automated content analysis step ("algorithmic error") as one link in a chain, and says explicitly that machine-learned labels are error-prone measurements, not truth. Asad's MSc work already produces the two ingredients this needs: a fine-tuned BERT with macro-F1 0.878 and a human validation set with Cohen's kappa ~0.78, i.e. a quantified misclassification matrix. The unpublished manuscript with Dr Ella Haig goes further — generalizability theory, measurement invariance, DIF and score linking applied to machine-labelled sentiment, with a 500-replication simulation showing a pooled cross-platform index reports the wrong sign of aggregate sentiment on ~25% of days. SP1 pools news outlet content with donated feeds from different platforms and asks about "the amount and character of negativity" across them; that is exactly the pooling operation his simulation shows can invert. Proposing to carry a negativity classifier's error rate through to the donation-level estimate, and to test measurement invariance of a negativity scale across outlet text, Facebook feed items and TikTok captions, is a defensible, non-obvious contribution to SP1 that no paper found here has done.
*Use in: research proposal — this is the novelty claim*

### Willingness to donate is high and actual donation is low, and the gap is technical, not attitudinal. The best-documented Facebook funnel is Keusch et al. 2024: 79% willing, 48% of the willing succeeded, and 83% of failures blamed technical problems.
Keusch, F., Pankowska, P. K., Cernat, A., & Bach, R. L. (2024). "Do You Have Two Minutes to Talk about Your Data? Willingness to Participate and Nonparticipation Bias in Facebook Data Donation." Field Methods 36(4), 279–293. DOI 10.1177/1525822X231225907. German online access panel; 1,092 surveyed, 913 proceeded to the donation task; 725 (79%) willing; 722 packages received, 684 linkable to 345 participants — so 48% of the willing successfully donated at least one of the two requested DDPs. Of 41 who explained non-donation, 83% cited a technical problem downloading, unzipping or uploading. Of the 140 unwilling, 24% cited privacy, 20% fear of misuse, 14% anticipated technical problems. A gain-vs-loss framing experiment on the request had no effect (chi-square = .024, p = .876) — message framing does not fix this. Women were 7 percentage points less willing; trust in researchers raised both willingness (+1 p.p. per scale point) and success (+2 p.p. per point); lower trust in Facebook lowered success (−3 p.p. per point). They used the OSD2F platform and warn their panel figure is "an upper bound" for a general population.
*Use in: both — the proposal's justification for hub-assisted donation; the application's evidence that Asad understands the real failure mode is engineering*

### The published evidence points at physical, assisted donation as the fix — but no published study has actually run a public data-donation hub. SDU's hubs are the unsolved part, and the proposal can say so.
Van Driel et al. 2022 conclude that the drop-out and corrupt-package problems "could be solved with face-to-face or live video instructions. But participants would need to be visited twice, once to instruct them how to download the DDPs and once again to share their DDP with the researcher" — the two-visit problem, caused by asynchronous DDP delivery. Carrière et al. 2024 mention only in passing that researchers can tailor design "by assisting participants in a lab or through online meetings". Welbers, Loecherbach, Lin & Trilling (2024), Computational Communication Research 6(2), 1–28, DOI 10.5117/CCR2024.2.5.WELB, is the closest published comparison: an open-source donation app tested in both a survey study and a field lab, concluding that "drop-out is high and non-random in the survey study" but that "a field lab setting might help alleviate primary barriers of participation". Searches for donation booths, donation stations, roadshows, festival or library donation setups returned no published empirical study. The literature is unanimous that assistance should help and has no evidence on how much, for whom, or at what cost — which is precisely what SDU's hubs will generate.
*Use in: research proposal — frame the hub as the method contribution, not just infrastructure to be babysat*

### The two-visit problem may now be obsolete, and that is a concrete, checkable design proposal for the hubs: TikTok delivers DDPs instantaneously, Instagram and YouTube in 10–15 minutes.
Karnam, S. K., Dash, A., Das, A., Mousavi, S., Bechtold, S., Gummadi, K. P., Mukherjee, A., Weber, I., & Zannettou, S., "Setting the Course, but Forgetting to Steer: Analyzing Compliance with GDPR's Right of Access to Data by Instagram, TikTok, and YouTube", arXiv:2502.11208, accepted at IEEE Symposium on Security and Privacy 2026. They measure request effort and turnaround: TikTok 6 clicks and instantaneous delivery; Instagram 6 clicks, 10–15 minutes; YouTube 10 clicks, 10–15 minutes. Compare Carrière et al. 2024, which still reports "WhatsApp account information takes three days" and "Google DDPs takes between an hour and three days", and Van Driel et al. 2022, where Instagram took up to 48 hours. If turnaround for the platforms SP1 cares about is now minutes, a single-visit hub becomes feasible: request, coffee, extract, inspect, consent, donate, leave — collapsing the delivery-latency attrition that de León et al. identify as the dominant mechanism. This is a testable claim a PhD student should verify per platform in Denmark rather than assert.
*Use in: research proposal — the operational design of the hub*

### Attrition in donation is driven by how long the platform takes to deliver the package, not by privacy fear — de León et al. show this experimentally, and it is the argument for a hub.
de León, E., Boeschoten, L., Votta, F., Mulder, J., Struminskaya, B., Oberski, D., Araujo, T., & de Vreese, C. (2026). "How Much Data Should I Request? Balancing Richness and Compliance in Digital Trace Data Donations." Social Science Computer Review, advance online, DOI 10.1177/08944393261435088. Randomised experiment on Facebook and Instagram donations: control asked for the default 1-year window, treatment for the entire account history. Participants asked for more data were less likely to complete. From the abstract: "we propose that this is not primarily due to heightened privacy concerns, but rather because these data packages are significantly larger and therefore take longer for the platforms to deliver. This additional time to deliver data packages results in increased attrition." They add a measurement point directly relevant to a negativity study: the chosen time window "fundamentally redefines what key constructs represent, potentially transforming intended static indicators into narrow snapshots of recent behavior". For SP1 this means the choice of request window is not admin, it is construct definition — a one-week Instagram watch history measures something different from a year of Facebook.
*Use in: both*

### What is actually inside a DDP differs enormously by platform, and for the negativity question the crucial gap is that DDPs record what users DID, not what they SAW.
Two sources. Karnam et al. (IEEE S&P 2026), on retention windows: YouTube watch history covers the entire account lifetime with URL, timestamp, title and author; Instagram gives only the last ~2 weeks and omits URLs and titles; TikTok gives roughly 6 months with URLs and timestamps. YouTube omits like history from the DDP entirely. Instagram videos watched under Reels are missing even when likes on them are recorded. Reliability: TikTok 100% complete for browsing and likes; YouTube 99.5% complete; Instagram 91% overall correctness with timestamps drifting up to ±60 seconds, 6% data loss between repeat requests, and coverage varying between 6 and 13 days. None of the three discloses purpose of collection, retention period, or third-party recipients. Second source: Hase, V., Ausloos, J., Boeschoten, L. et al. (2024), "Fulfilling data access obligations: How could (and should) platforms facilitate data donation studies?", Internet Policy Review 13(3), DOI 10.14763/2024.3.1793, which documents 14 challenges across 12 data donation projects, grouped as accessibility (I–VII), transparency (VIII–XI) and completeness (XII–XIV). Challenge XII is missing activity/exposure data; they state flatly that "data donation studies cannot study exposure to misinformation on digital platforms, as exposure data is often missing from DDPs". Challenge XIII: YouTube DDPs "contain links to or IDs of accounts that platform users follow rather than account names"; TikTok gives "links to watched content instead of video titles"; Instagram gives IDs not titles. Challenge XIV: DDPs do not say whether content was algorithmically recommended or encountered elsewhere. Their four recommendations to platforms: one central access link with machine-readable JSON/CSV and a minimum 30-day download window; public documentation of what is collected and how it is measured; complete datasets including exposure and context; and user-authorised researcher data-access mandates.
*Use in: research proposal — this is the hard methodological constraint SP1 must design around, and naming it will read as expertise*

### The ID-not-title problem is where seven years of production web data collection becomes a research method, not a CV line — a donated feed of bare URLs and video IDs is unanalysable for negativity until it is resolved back to content, and the APIs that used to do that are gone.
Hase et al. 2024 state the consequence directly: researchers must supplement DDPs with APIs, but "most platforms shut down or drastically restrict their APIs", rendering donation studies unfeasible for content analysis. SP1 requires "automated content analyses" of "individual social media feeds" — which means every donated YouTube ID, TikTok link and Instagram media ID has to be resolved to text, caption or transcript before any negativity classifier can touch it, at population scale, with breakage-tolerant retries, and lawfully. Asad's 68 collectors ingesting 10 platforms daily with an automated repair layer at M+C Saatchi Fluency, the earlier 500+ collectors across 15 countries at Dubizzle Labs, and web-scraping-guide.com on anti-bot systems and agentic browsers, are the direct answer to a problem the field names as blocking. Note the ethical framing: this must be presented as resolving content the donor already consented to, under SDU governance, not as open-web scraping.
*Use in: motivated application — this is the strongest differentiating claim against a pure comm-science applicant*

### Package format instability is the recurring engineering failure, and the field has no automated defence — every account of it is manual firefighting.
Van Driel, I. I., Giachanou, A., Pouwels, J. L., Boeschoten, L., Beyens, I., & Valkenburg, P. M. (2022), "Promises and Pitfalls of Social Media Data Donations", Communication Methods and Measures 16(4), 266–282, DOI 10.1080/19312458.2022.2109608: "the composition of the DDPs depends on the time of donation", new folders and files "are typically not rolled out simultaneously across all users", so "the structure and content of the DDP is not stable across time and participants". Their Figure 1 compares the same Instagram story in a 2020 DDP (media folders plus JSON) and a 2022 DDP (text files inside folders, with a new JSON-or-HTML choice). Carrière et al. 2024 add that Facebook DDPs come as .json or .html, WhatsApp DDPs are structurally different on Android versus Apple including timestamp notation and media handling, and JSON key labels differ by the user's language setting — the last being lethal for a Danish study where donors' phones may be set to Danish or English. Gómez Ortega 2025 documents Garmin's file structure shifting mid-campaign between 9 and 21 June 2023, Google exports changing with account language, empty files from a policy change, and describes maintaining the scripts through it as "invisible labor". Hase et al. call it Challenge XI: platforms alter DDP structures with no advance notice and no documentation. Nobody proposes a solution beyond "test with varied setups" — an automated repair layer with contract tests over golden DDP fixtures and alerting on schema drift is an obvious, unclaimed contribution.
*Use in: both*

### Self-selection is real and, worse for a negativity study, it is correlated with the outcome: donors self-report less news and political social media use than non-donors, and political leaning predicts donation.
"The platform matters: cross-platform differences in data donation willingness, behavior, and bias", Communication Methods and Measures, published online 27 December 2025, DOI 10.1080/19312458.2025.2605946. German non-probability quota sample, N = 2,296, covering YouTube, Facebook, Instagram and TikTok, yielding 340 donated packages. Participants were least likely to donate TikTok data. Gender was the main driver of drop-off at the willingness step; political leaning predicted donation at the donation step for every platform except Facebook. Most damaging for SP1: "data donors tend to self-report less active social media usage with news and political content than those who do not donate data". Compare Van Driel et al. 2022, where donors were more often girls (68% vs 52%, chi-square(2, N = 388) = 14.03, p = .001) and less often on a lower educational track (31% vs 48%, chi-square = 9.23, p = .01), with no age difference. A proposal that measures the amount of negative news in Danish feeds without a weighting or linkage strategy is measuring a self-selected, less news-engaged slice — the fix in Boeschoten et al. is to link donations to a parallel survey so the missingness can be modelled, which SP2 and SP3's Danish survey infrastructure makes available.
*Use in: research proposal — the representativeness section*

### Partial donation is a distinct, quantifiable bias: donors are allowed to delete entries before donating, and they do — concentrated among first-time donors.
Wedel, L., & Ohme, J. (2026). "Longitudinal Data Donation Behavior and Data Omission across Four Social Media Platforms." Computational Communication Research 8(1), DOI 10.5117/CCR2026.1.3.WEDE. Two-wave longitudinal study across TikTok, YouTube, Facebook and Instagram. Findings: platform donor rate rose in wave 2 but with substantial donor attrition between waves; omission of data traces is "predominantly observed among first-time donors"; and allowing selective omission "significantly compromises data completeness", so the trade-off needs careful weighing. This is the empirical content of Boeschoten et al.'s consent error. It also implies a hub-specific design question: does a face-to-face setting increase or decrease deletion before donation? Trust in the researcher goes up (Keusch et al. show trust in researchers predicts success), but so possibly does felt observation. That is a clean, cheap experiment to embed in the hubs.
*Use in: research proposal*

### The tooling to build on is settled and open source, and it runs Python in the participant's browser — which means the extraction and negativity-preprocessing code Asad writes is the same Python he already writes.
Three generations. (1) OSD2F: Araujo, T., de Vreese, C., van de Velde, B., Trilling, D., Ohme, J., Moeller, J., Loecherbach, F., van Atteveldt, W., Ausloos, J., & Welbers, K. (2022), "OSD2F: An Open-Source Data Donation Framework", Computational Communication Research 4(2), DOI 10.5117/CCR2022.2.001.ARAU; github.com/uvacw/osd2f, YAML-configured whitelisting of JSON fields, aiming to stay "export agnostic", archived 20 May 2024 with maintenance moved to Port. Keusch et al.'s Facebook study ran on OSD2F, and Erik Knudsen's MediaFutures group at Bergen forked it for Norwegian media research (github.com/sfimediafutures/Data-Donation) — the nearest Nordic precedent to what SDU is building. (2) Port: Boeschoten, L., de Schipper, N. C., Mendrik, A. M., van der Veen, E., Struminskaya, B., Janssen, H., & Araujo, T. (2023), "Port: A software tool for digital data donation", Journal of Open Source Software 8(90), 5596, DOI 10.21105/joss.05596 — uses Pyodide to run the researcher's Python extraction script inside the participant's browser so the raw DDP never leaves the device. (3) The current D3I stack at datadonation.eu, funded by PDI-SSH across six Dutch universities with Eyra: a data-donation-task front end forked from Feldspar, driven by Python scripts under packages/python/port/platforms/, shipping support for LinkedIn, Instagram, Facebook, YouTube, TikTok, Netflix, ChatGPT, WhatsApp, X and Chrome, deployed on Eyra's Next platform (self-hosted, SURF Research Cloud, or SaaS). Carrière et al. name Port as the reference implementation.
*Use in: both — cite the stack by name in the proposal; in the application, note that the extraction layer is Python and browser-side, i.e. his existing toolchain*

### There is a six-step workflow paper that reads as a job description for this post, and it names five kinds of expertise the project needs — Asad plausibly covers two of them that comm-science candidates do not.
Carrière, T. C., Boeschoten, L., Struminskaya, B., et al. (2024/2025). "Best practices for studies using digital data donation." Quality & Quantity 59(Suppl 1), 389–412. DOI 10.1007/s11135-024-01983-x, open access at PMC11971172. Workflow: define the donation purpose and evaluate candidate platforms; design the study and participant flow; develop robust feature-extraction scripts; configure the donation tool; obtain ethical approval via DPIA, privacy policy and consent forms; run a pilot before fielding. It states that five domains of expertise are needed — applied researchers, methodology experts, research engineers, IT specialists, and data protection legal experts. The advert's phrasing, that "a crucial task for the PhD student will be to manage the infrastructure of the so-called data donation hubs", maps onto research engineer and IT specialist. Other specifics worth quoting: platforms must answer an access request within 30 days but in practice range from minutes to days; download windows expire (Facebook and TikTok four days, WhatsApp 30) so a participant who misses the window has to re-request and often quits; extraction failures such as an unrecognised date-time notation cause partial collection; consent is effectively two-stage (enrol, then consent again after inspecting the extract); and privacy assurance must be stated "explicitly and repeatedly" because in two of their own illustrations participants withdrew believing privacy was not guaranteed.
*Use in: both — the proposal's work plan can literally follow these six steps; the application should mirror the expertise vocabulary*

### The de-identification step actively destroys the signal a negativity study needs, and nobody has solved it — this is a second unclaimed contribution.
Van Driel et al. 2022 describe their pipeline (script archived at Zenodo, DOI 10.5281/zenodo.5211335): whole files such as "uploaded contacts" and "devices" removed; names, phone numbers, e-mail addresses and URLs replaced with _name, _phone number, _URL; usernames replaced by participant numbers; faces in videos and username text in images covered. Then the cost: "all words following a tag were replaced by a code", which also removed company handles like @Nike, influencer handles and locations like @home; and "by covering all faces in images, studying emotions that are shared through facial expressions is impossible". For SP1 this is acute — if the tag following @ is the news outlet, blanket handle redaction erases the outlet identity that the whole "which outlets are more negative" question depends on. A selective-redaction design (public-entity allowlist for news brands and verified accounts, redaction only of private-person handles) validated against re-identification risk is a real methodological problem with a tractable answer. They also flag two ethical problems with no clean solution: third parties in the donor's data cannot consent (tags, DMs, uploaded contacts), and donors may have used the account when under the platform's legal age.
*Use in: research proposal*

### When donation is too burdensome, the field's current best answer is to stop asking for the package at all — an important sanity check on scope, and a fallback if the hub underperforms.
Asensio, M., Bosch, O. J., & Roberts, C. (2025). "What is the best way of collecting data donations? An experiment assessing the feasibility of different data donation approaches to measure mobile and app usage." Information, Communication & Society, published online 25 October 2025, DOI 10.1080/1369118X.2025.2570738. A 2 × 3 between-subjects web survey experiment, N = 872, in a research-led probability-based panel in Switzerland; participants were randomised to capture and share their data via screenshots, via video recordings, or by manually typing their log figures ("enhanced recall"). Compliance was very low for screenshots and video recordings; almost two thirds donated via enhanced recall. Their conclusion is that enhanced recall is the most feasible approach for mobile and app usage, with the caveat that it needs testing for longer and more burdensome tasks. The relevance to SP1 is as a boundary condition: enhanced recall cannot deliver feed content for automated negativity coding, so SP1 has no fallback but the real DDP — which is exactly why the hub matters.
*Use in: background only*

## LINE: measuring-negativity

### The canonical validation benchmark in communication science found that NO sentiment dictionary reaches acceptable validity on news — including the Lexicoder dictionary Soroka built for exactly this purpose.
van Atteveldt, van der Velden & Boukes (2021), Communication Methods and Measures 15(2):121-140. Gold standard of 300 Dutch economic news headlines, triple-coded, drawn from 6,038 manually coded headlines (Boukes et al. 2020). Krippendorff's ordinal alpha against the gold standard: manual coding by 3 trained students 0.90 (acc .88); single student 0.82; crowd coding 3-5 coders 0.81; CNN 0.50 (acc .63); SVM 0.41; Naive Bayes 0.39. Then every dictionary collapses: Hu & Liu 0.34 (best), LSD/Lexicoder 0.33 (acc .46), NRC 0.32 (Dutch) / 0.23 (English), AFINN 0.27, Loughran-McDonald 0.29, General Inquirer 0.26, Pattern 0.07, RID 0.06. Bivariate correlation with the gold standard: LSD 0.34, Hu&Liu 0.34, NRC 0.33, RID 0.11. Their verdict, verbatim from the paper: dictionaries 'all measure something else, and none of them can be seen as a valid measurement.' The failure mode is diagnosed precisely — dictionaries have decent PRECISION but catastrophic RECALL (Damstra & Boukes precision 0.83, recall 0.07), i.e. type II errors. Error analysis: Naive Bayes learned 'bankruptcy' and 'unemployment' as POSITIVE features because they are usually negated in Danish-style news phrasing; the CNN classified 'more bankruptcies' as positive. Note the paper predates fine-tuned transformers — its best model is a CNN, which is the opening this proposal can walk through.
*Use in: both — this is the single most important citation for the research proposal's problem statement, and the number (LSD alpha 0.33) is the hook*

### Off-the-shelf sentiment tools do not even agree with EACH OTHER on news, so tool choice silently determines the finding — but aggregating over time partially rescues validity, which is precisely the argument of Asad's unpublished manuscript with Dr Haig.
Boukes, van de Velde, Araujo & Vliegenthart (2020), Communication Methods and Measures 14(2):83-104, 'What's the Tone? Easy Doesn't Do It'. Dutch economic news, 4,640 headlines and 1,426 full texts. Human coders were reliable (alpha .80 headlines, .69 full texts); every tool was not: best were Polyglot (alpha .25/.26) and LIWC (.23/.35), SentiStrength .18/.16, Pattern .17/.09, a 'recession' keyword count -.01. Agreement BETWEEN tools was also alpha < .35; Pattern vs SentiStrength alpha .09. Their conclusion: 'scholars could reach substantially different conclusions if they use different off-the-shelf dictionaries' on the same data. Crucially for Asad: Table 6 shows correlation with human coding rises sharply with temporal aggregation — LIWC on headlines goes r = .26 (per article) to .41 (per day) to .60 (per week); Damstra & Boukes on full text r = .26 to .36 to .63; combined approach r = .34 to .47 to .52. But this only works if article-level agreement is decent to begin with (Pattern and DANEW get WORSE, DANEW full-text going .18 to -.15 at week level). This is the exact same aggregation/measurement-error structure as Asad's 500-replication simulation showing a pooled cross-platform index reports the wrong sign of daily aggregate sentiment ~25% of days — he can cite Boukes as the precedent and position his simulation as the formal treatment of when aggregation rescues versus when it deceives.
*Use in: both — the strongest bridge between the published literature and Asad's own unpublished work*

### 'Negativity' is not one construct, and the advert's phrase 'amount AND CHARACTER of negativity' is a direct reference to that multidimensionality — a proposal that offers a single sentiment score has misread the call.
Lengauer, Esser & Berganza (2012), 'Negativity in political news: A review of concepts, operationalizations and key findings', Journalism 13(2):179-202. They argue negativity has not reached the status of a homogeneous overarching concept and propose three orthogonal distinctions: (1) negativity versus CONFRONTATION (tone versus conflict framing), (2) FRAME-related negativity versus individual ACTOR-related negativity, (3) NON-DIRECTIONAL versus DIRECTIONAL negativity (is it just negative, or negative toward a specified target). This maps onto the separate, and stronger, finding from Soroka, Young & Balmas (2015), 'Bad News or Mad News? Sentiment Scoring of Negativity, Fear, and Anger in News Content', ANNALS of the AAPSS 659(1):108-121: across roughly 55,000 front-page news stories they compared four affect lexicons and showed fear and anger are DISTINCT dimensions capturing different sentiments, with negativity more strongly related to anger than to fear. Practical implication for SP1: the deliverable should be a multidimensional negativity profile (valence, discrete emotion, conflict/confrontation, target-directedness), not a scalar. Asad's ArtemisAI stack already has separate fine-tuned sentiment AND emotion classifiers plus Detoxify, which is structurally the right shape for this.
*Use in: research proposal — this is how to make the two-page proposal read as theoretically anchored rather than purely technical*

### Fine-tuned transformers DO clear the validity bar that dictionaries fail, and this has been demonstrated in a non-English European political language — this is the affirmative methodological argument for the proposal.
Widmann & Wich (2023), 'Creating and Comparing Dictionary, Word Embedding, and Transformer-Based Models to Measure Discrete Emotions in German Political Text', Political Analysis 31(4):626-641. They built and compared three tools for eight discrete emotions (anger, fear, disgust, sadness, joy, enthusiasm, pride, hope) in German political text: the purpose-built ED8 dictionary, locally trained word embeddings plus neural classifiers, and an ELECTRA transformer. Roughly 10,000 crowd-coded sentences (about 9,898 after quality control), 90/10 train/test. Verbatim from the abstract: 'the results highlight the strengths of novel transformer-based models' and 'all customized approaches outperform widely used off-the-shelf dictionaries in measuring emotional language in German political discourse.' Models and code are public at github.com/tweedmann/3x8emotions. The template Asad should copy explicitly: build a purpose-annotated gold standard in the target language, fine-tune an encoder, and validate all three families against it rather than assuming the transformer wins.
*Use in: research proposal — the methodological blueprint, and a direct analogue (German : Danish) to propose replicating*

### The best-known large-scale transformer study of news headline negativity is also a cautionary tale: its emotion classifier scored 39% accuracy and it still reported a '104% increase in anger'.
Rozado, Hughes & Halberstadt (2022), PLOS ONE. 23 million headlines from 47 US news outlets, 2000-2019, average 58.3 characters. Sentiment labelled with SiEBERT (RoBERTa-large fine-tuned on 15 datasets): 75% accuracy against human annotators whose own interrater agreement was 80%. Emotion labelled with a DistilRoBERTa-base fine-tuned for Ekman categories plus neutral: 39% accuracy, against human agreement of 36%. Headline findings: a -314% change in average sentiment 2000-2019 (t(18) = -9.63, p < 10^-7), structural break around 2010, right-leaning outlets more negative throughout; anger +104%, fear +150%, sadness +54%, disgust +29%, neutral -30%. The critique Asad can make and then solve: a 39%-accurate instrument cannot support a claimed 104% trend without an explicit error model, because classifier error that is itself correlated with time or outlet propagates straight into the trend estimate. This is exactly the measurement-invariance/DIF question from his manuscript with Dr Haig, transplanted from platforms to outlets and time.
*Use in: both — a named weakness in the state of the art that Asad's specific expertise repairs*

### There is no validated Danish measure of news negativity. Every existing Danish sentiment tool is benchmarked on tweets and parliamentary speech, on evaluation sets of 184 to 512 sentences — and the main Danish NLP toolkit was archived in 2025.
From DaNLP's own benchmark table (alexandrainst/danlp, docs/tasks/sentiment_analysis.md), accuracy with macro-F1 in brackets: AFINN 0.68 (0.68) Europarl / 0.66 (0.61) LCC / 0.48 (0.46) Twitter; Sentida 0.67 (0.65) / 0.58 (0.55) / 0.44 (0.44); BERT Tone 0.79 (0.78) / 0.74 (0.67) / 0.73 (0.70); spaCy sentiment 0.74 / 0.66 / 0.66; Senda (built by Ekstra Bladet) 0.75 (0.74) / 0.68 (0.59) / 0.76 (0.73). The evaluation sets are tiny: Europarl Sentiment 184 sentences, LCC Sentiment 499 sentences, Twitter Sentiment 512 evaluation examples. BERT Tone was fine-tuned on Twitter plus EuroParl and is built on BotXO's Danish BERT. DaNLP's BERT Emotion (8 emotions, trained on Danmarks Radio Facebook data) scores 0.65 accuracy / 0.64 macro-F1 on 999 examples. Critically: the DaNLP GitHub repository is now ARCHIVED (verified via GitHub API, last push 12 Feb 2025), so it must be treated as a legacy resource. DaCy by contrast is actively maintained (last push 10 September 2026). Not one of these is validated on Danish news articles. That gap IS the contribution SP1 needs.
*Use in: both — this is the single strongest 'here is the hole and I can fill it' argument available for this application*

### SENTIDA, the most-cited native Danish sentiment tool, was validated on 45 self-annotated texts and never on news — I verified this against the paper after a summarizer gave me fabricated figures.
Lauridsen, Dalsgaard & Svendsen (2019), 'SENTIDA: A New Tool for Sentiment Analysis in Danish', Journal of Language Works 4(1). Built by merging the AFINN Danish lexicon (3,552 rated words, but only 3,089 unique once inflections are removed) with the 10,000 most frequent Danish lemmas from Det Danske Sprog- og Litteraturselskab; 9,779 lemmas annotated independently by the three authors on an 11-point scale; stemmed down to 5,263 entries detecting roughly 35,000 Danish word forms. Validation 1: binary classification of Trustpilot reviews, 10 trials, SENTIDA mean accuracy 0.81 (SD 0.01) versus AFINN 0.745 (SD 0.02); Spearman correlation with star ratings SENTIDA 0.63 vs AFINN 0.56. Validation 2, the domain test: only 45 texts total, 15 each from fiction, political texts and social media, mean length 37.3 words, annotated by six people INCLUDING THE AUTHORS. Correlations: politics 0.72, fiction 0.74, social media 0.86. The paper's own limitations section concedes the corpora 'were made by the authors' and that '15 texts in each domain are not enough to entirely validate'. News is named as an aspiration ('everything from poems to news articles') but was never tested. Separately, Enevoldsen & Hansen (2017) applied AFINN to 360 articles from Berlingske and Information and concluded AFINN is unsuited to news because it was built for microblogging and rates obscenities that never appear in news copy.
*Use in: research proposal — lets Asad show he has actually read the Danish tooling rather than name-dropping it*

### Danish pretraining data is structurally thin on news and its 'social media' is dominated by a single horse forum — a concrete domain-shift risk for a project that analyses news outlets AND donated social feeds.
Strømberg-Derczynski et al. (2021), 'The Danish Gigaword Corpus', NoDaLiDa 2021, aclanthology.org/2021.nodalida-main.46. Total 1,045 million words. Composition by domain: Legal 308.8M (29.55%), Social Media 261.4M (25.01%), Conversation 239.4M (22.91%), Web 101.0M (9.67%), Wiki & Books 92.2M (8.82%), NEWS 40.0M (3.92%), Other 1.2M. The entire news section is two sources: TV2R 2015-2019 (10M words) and DanAvis 1999-2003 (30M words). The social media section is 228.9M words of Hestenettet (a horse forum) plus 32.0M of Twitter plus 0.5M of election tweets. The paper also documents that multilingual FastText embeddings miss core Danish words such as 'træls' and that multilingual BERT lacks sufficient support for the Danish vowel 'å'. Implication to state plainly in the proposal: an encoder pretrained on this distribution has seen very little contemporary Danish news and almost no Instagram/TikTok/YouTube-style feed text, so domain-adaptive pretraining on the project's own Infomedia news pull and donated feed text is a justified, budgetable work package rather than an optional extra.
*Use in: research proposal — a specific, checkable technical risk with a specific mitigation, which is what a methods section should contain*

### The best-performing Danish NLP pipeline is built on XLM-RoBERTa — the exact model family Asad has already fine-tuned in production at ArtemisAI.
Enevoldsen, Hansen & Nielbo (2021), 'DaCy: A Unified Framework for Danish NLP', arXiv:2107.05295, Aarhus University Center for Humanities Computing. DaCy large is XLM-RoBERTa: POS accuracy 98.39, NER average F1 85.20 (PER 95.53, LOC 83.90, ORG 77.82, MISC 80.16), UAS 90.59. DaCy medium is DaBERT (NER avg F1 78.47), DaCy small is Ælæctra Cased (76.55). They evaluate robustness by augmenting the DaNE test set with keystroke errors (2/5/15% of characters), æøå-to-ae/oe/aa substitution, and lowercasing, and report that all models except DaCy large 'display significant biases related to ethnicity while only Polyglot shows a significant gender bias' — they argue augmentation-based evaluation is especially valuable 'for languages with limited benchmark sets'. Corroborating from ScandEval (Nielsen 2023, NoDaLiDa, arXiv:2304.00906): the best Danish model overall is DFM-encoder-large-v1 from chcaa/Danish Foundation Models, ahead of DanskBERT, RemBERT and mDeBERTaV3; the Danish sentiment task is AngryTweets (crowdsourced Danish tweets); and cross-lingual transfer is strong within Mainland Scandinavian (a Danish-trained model scores 63.87 on Danish, 53.74 Norwegian, 52.08 Swedish, but only 30.39 Icelandic), F-statistic 16.81 for the da+no pair. Two usable moves for Asad: (a) his XLM-R fine-tuning experience transfers directly and he should say so; (b) the augmentation-as-validation method is a ready-made robustness protocol for a small Danish gold standard, and donated feed text is exactly where casing and spelling noise will bite.
*Use in: motivated application — the cleanest 'my existing production skills are the skills this project needs' line, with DaCy's augmentation protocol as a proposal-ready technique*

### The LLM shortcut for a non-Danish speaker is real but unproven for Danish specifically, and SDU's own computer scientists published a Jan 2026 benchmark showing frontier models handle Danish-specific content poorly.
The promise: Rathje, Mirea, Sucholutsky, Marjieh, Robertson & Van Bavel (2024), 'GPT is an effective tool for multilingual psychological text analysis', PNAS 121(34), e2308950121. GPT-3.5/4/4-Turbo detected sentiment, discrete emotions, offensiveness and moral foundations across 12 languages using 15 datasets and 47,925 manually annotated tweets and news headlines, with no training data. GPT correlated r = 0.59-0.77 with human annotators versus r = 0.20-0.30 for English dictionary analysis, and approached fine-tuned models (GPT-4 Turbo F1 0.782 on English discrete emotions vs fine-tuned BERT 0.785). BUT the 12 languages are English, Arabic, Indonesian, Turkish, Swahili, Hausa, Amharic, Yoruba, Igbo, Twi, Kinyarwanda and Tsonga — DANISH IS NOT AMONG THEM, and the news-headline dataset was only 213 items. The counterweight, and it is local: Nielsen, Beltoft, Schneider-Kamp & Galke Poech (2026), 'SDUs DAISY: A Benchmark for Danish Culture', arXiv:2601.19930, all four authors at University of Southern Denmark. 741 human-approved question-answer pairs built from the 2006 Danish Culture Canon. Baseline results are poor across the board: Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct best with BLEU 0.166 / F1 0.268, GPT-OSS-120B 0.126 / 0.211, Mistral-Small-3.1-24B 0.124 / 0.202, Gemma-3-27B 0.123 / 0.193, GPT-OSS-20B 0.062 / 0.112. They write that models 'generally exhibit poor performance on Daisy, despite that it is very likely that data from Danish Wikipedia was included in their training data', and attribute it to limited Denmark-specific content in training data. The honest position for the proposal: LLM-assisted annotation is a promising first-pass labeller for Danish that MUST itself be validated against a human Danish gold standard before use — which is a research contribution, not a caveat.
*Use in: both — this is how Asad turns 'I don't speak Danish' into a methodological research question, and DAISY gives him an SDU-internal citation and a potential internal collaborator*

### A realistic division of labour for a non-Danish speaker already exists in the literature as a named problem, and the workflow that solves it plays to Asad's exact strengths.
Baden, Pipal, Schoonvelde & van der Velden (2022), 'Three Gaps in Computational Text Analysis Methods for Social Sciences: A Research Agenda', Communication Methods and Measures 16(1):1-18. Gap three is stated as the dominance of English-language tools depressing comparative research and inclusivity toward scholarly communities working in other languages; gap one is that method development has prioritised technological over validity concerns and given limited attention to operationalising social-scientific measurement. The workable split for this PhD, which Asad should describe explicitly rather than hide: Danish-native colleagues (Kim Andersen, Lene Heiselberg, David Nicolas Hopmann, Miriam Brems, plus student coders) own codebook construction and gold-standard annotation in Danish; Asad owns the codebook-to-classifier pipeline, the data donation hub infrastructure, the fine-tuning, and — the genuinely novel part — the measurement-validity layer: inter-coder reliability targets, learning curves for how much Danish annotation is actually needed, per-outlet and per-platform DIF testing, score linking so news-outlet and donated-feed negativity scores are on a comparable scale, and error propagation into the aggregate estimates SP2 and SP3 will consume. Two concrete supports: van Atteveldt et al.'s own recommendation is to annotate a gold standard coded by at least two coders and use roughly 100+ items per Krippendorff's Table 11.2 guidance, and their learning curve shows SVM at ~50% accuracy with 2,000 training documents rising to ~55% at 4,000 — so annotation budget is an empirical question the PhD can answer for Danish. Practical corpus note: Danish news for SP1 realistically comes via Infomedia, the largest Scandinavian media archive covering Danish print, broadcast and online from 1975 onwards with API access; per DTU Library, Infomedia merged with Retriever and access moved to Retriever as of 16 April 2026.
*Use in: motivated application — this is the paragraph that neutralises the obvious objection to his candidacy*

### VERDICT: the claim is partially occupied. The premise (automated sentiment measures do not transfer across domains) is textbook canon in exactly the journal Andersen's team publishes in; the specific psychometric operationalisation (invariance/DIF/linking applied to machine labels across outlet vs platform vs individual feed) is not occupied.
van Atteveldt, van der Velden & Boukes (2021), Communication Methods and Measures 15(2), 121-140, doi 10.1080/19312458.2020.1869198. I extracted the PDF text. They write: 'the validity of sentiment analysis highly depends on the domain, genre and language to which it is applied'; 'All automated methods... will decrease in performance when applied to a task or domain that is different from the one it was developed for'; and in the recommendations, 'Since tasks and domains are almost never interchangeable in social science, validated performance on the task a tool was developed for does not guarantee sufficient performance on a new task.' Their study: Dutch economic news headlines, gold standard coded multiple times; trained students reach Krippendorff's alpha above 0.9 with three coders; crowd coding above 0.8; dictionary agreement 'generally close to chance agreement'; a CNN scored around 20 percentage points above dictionaries. Asad must NOT present 'the instrument may not travel' as his discovery. He must present it as the field's own accepted premise that the field has not yet turned into a testable measurement model.

Chan, Bajjalieh, Auvil, Wessler, Althaus, Welbers, van Atteveldt & Jungblut (2021), 'Four best practices for measuring news sentiment using off-the-shelf dictionaries: a large-scale p-hacking experiment', Computational Communication Research 3(1), 1-27, doi 10.5117/CCR2021.1.001.CHAN. I extracted the PDF. Corpus: 2,246,177 New York Times articles, June 1980 to January 2006; 37 sentiment scores (GI 2, LIWC 6, Bing Liu 1, ANEW 3, DAL 3, MFD 10, NRC 10, LSD 2). Only 40 of 91 positive-score pairs (43.9%) and 51 of 105 negative-score pairs (48.6%) correlate above r = 0.1. The first factor from exploratory factor analysis correlates r = -0.933 with article length. Article length is itself a Granger cause of presidential approval (p<0.001). They also warn that 'many sentiment scores actually indicate topics', so 'news articles on some topics (e.g. conflict events) will then automatically have higher sentiment scores than other topics, purely due to the ways some dictionaries are constructed.' This is Asad's argument at 90 percent strength, but held within ONE outlet and across INSTRUMENTS. His version holds ONE instrument and varies the CONTEXT (outlet, platform, individual feed). The document-length finding is the single most dangerous fact for him: length is precisely what differs between a news article, a Facebook post and a TikTok caption, so a reviewer will ask why cross-platform negativity differences are not simply Chan's length artefact. He should say so first and make length an explicitly modelled facet.
*Use in: research proposal*

### The general statistical result that imperfect machine labels bias downstream conclusions is already proven, twice, and rigorously. Asad cannot claim it. He can only claim the cross-context, differential version of it.
(a) Egami, Hinck, Stewart & Wei (2023), 'Using Imperfect Surrogates for Downstream Inference: Design-based Supervised Learning for Social Science Applications of Large Language Models', NeurIPS 2023 (Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems 36), arXiv 2306.04746, v1 7 Jun 2023, v3 14 Jan 2024. Verbatim from the abstract: direct use of surrogate labels in downstream analyses 'leads to substantial bias and invalid confidence intervals, even with high surrogate accuracy of 80-90%.' They ship an R package, dsl. (b) Knox, Lucas & Cho (2022), 'Testing Causal Theories with Learned Proxies', Annual Review of Political Science, doi 10.1146/annurev-polisci-051120-111443: proxy-based inference 'occurs in over two-thirds of recent computational work in political science' and 'Imperfect proxies can reflect noise and contamination from other concepts, producing biased point estimates and standard errors.' The narrow claim left standing for Asad: both treat error as a single nuisance to be corrected within one estimation setting. Neither asks whether the error structure itself DIFFERS by context, which is the only condition under which a comparison of amount of negativity across outlets and platforms flips sign rather than merely attenuating.
*Use in: both*

### Communication already has the attenuation result for content analysis error, from a simulation, in a top journal. Asad's simulation must be positioned as the differential-error counterpart to it, not as its rediscovery.
Scharkow & Bachl (2016), 'How Measurement Error in Content Analysis and Self-Reported Media Use Leads to Minimal Media Effect Findings in Linkage Analyses: A Simulation Study', Political Communication, doi 10.1080/10584609.2016.1235640, 103 citations. Abstract: measurement error in both content analysis and media-use self-reports 'will typically lead to severely downward-biased effect estimates', demonstrated with a large Monte Carlo simulation. This is directly load-bearing for HEALTHY NEWS because SP1's negativity measure feeds SP3's panel-survey effects on well-being: if SP1's instrument is noisy, SP3 finds a null. Asad's contribution is the sharper case: non-differential error attenuates toward zero, differential error across contexts does not, and his 500-replication result (pooled index wrong sign on ~25% of days) is exactly the pathology Scharkow and Bachl's design cannot produce. Saying this explicitly turns his unpublished manuscript from a lone claim into the missing second half of a known result.
*Use in: both*

### The instrument-instability evidence base is old and large on the engineering side. Framed as 'sentiment tools disagree', the claim is fully occupied since 2015-2016.
(a) Ribeiro, Araújo, Gonçalves, Gonçalves & Benevenuto, 'SentiBench - a benchmark comparison of state-of-the-practice sentiment analysis methods', arXiv 1512.01818 (submitted 6 Dec 2015), published in EPJ Data Science, doi 10.1140/epjds/s13688-016-0085-1: 24 sentiment methods evaluated across 18 labelled datasets spanning social networks, product reviews, movie critiques and news; key conclusion quoted from the abstract, results 'highlight the extent to which the prediction performance of these methods varies considerably across datasets.' (b) González-Bailón & Paltoglou (2015), 'Signals of Public Opinion in Online Communication: A Comparison of Methods and Data Sources', ANNALS of the AAPSS 659(1), 95-107, doi 10.1177/0002716215572084. Chan et al. cite both as establishing the 'domain-specificity problem' by name. Asad should adopt the field's term (domain specificity) and then say what nobody has said: domain specificity has been treated as an accuracy problem to be engineered away, never as a measurement-equivalence problem that determines whether a between-context comparison is interpretable at all.
*Use in: research proposal*

### UNOCCUPIED, and this is the wedge: no one has applied measurement invariance, DIF, or score linking to the OUTPUT of an automated content-analysis instrument. The psychometric vocabulary and the computational-content-analysis vocabulary have not met.
Negative evidence, gathered by structured search rather than by reading: OpenAlex title-and-abstract search returns 27 works for '"measurement invariance" AND "content analysis"' and 25 for '"measurement invariance" AND (sentiment OR "text analysis")'. I inspected the top-cited results of both. Every single hit applies invariance to human SURVEY responses (Islamophobia scales, nativism, achievement emotions, criminal-thinking inventories); none applies it to machine-generated labels. arXiv full-text search for '"measurement invariance" AND "classifier"' returns only computer-vision transformation-invariance papers, nothing on measurement equivalence of text classifiers. OpenAlex '"generalizability theory" AND (text OR "content analysis" OR "machine learning")' returns 158 works, all psychometrics textbooks and education/writing assessment. The nearest crossover is Kennedy, Bacon, Sahn & von Vacano (2020), 'Measuring a hate speech spectrum with faceted Rasch item response theory and perspective-aware, explainable-by-design deep learning', arXiv 2009.10277, which uses IRT to model ANNOTATOR perspective and build a continuous construct, but does not test invariance of the resulting measure across platforms or genres. This is where Asad's proposal should plant its flag.
*Use in: both*

### UNOCCUPIED and directly relevant to the advert: the data-donation methods literature has thoroughly covered WHO donates and WHAT is missing, and has said essentially nothing about whether a content measure means the same thing across the platforms inside one person's donated feed.
The field-defining pieces are about sampling and infrastructure, not instrument equivalence. Wedel, Ohme, Mayer, Gaisbauer & Fan (2025), 'The platform matters: cross-platform differences in data donation willingness, behavior, and bias', Communication Methods and Measures, doi 10.1080/19312458.2025.2605946: German quota sample N = 2,296 across YouTube, Facebook, Instagram and TikTok, 340 donation packages analysed; TikTok data least likely to be donated; gender drives willingness, political leaning predicts donation for all platforms except Facebook. Companion: Wedel & Ohme (2026), 'Longitudinal Data Donation Behavior and Data Omission across Four Social Media Platforms', Computational Communication Research, accepted (dataset doi 10.34669/wi.rd/6). Also van Driel, Giachanou, Pouwels, Boeschoten et al. (2022), 'Promises and Pitfalls of Social Media Data Donations', Communication Methods and Measures, doi 10.1080/19312458.2022.2109608, 105 citations, based on 110 Instagram DDPs from 102 adolescents; and Ohme, Araujo, Boeschoten, Freelon et al. (2023), 'Digital Trace Data Collection for Social Media Effects Research: APIs, Data Donation, and (Screen) Tracking', Communication Methods and Measures, doi 10.1080/19312458.2023.2181319, 175 citations. Note the shape of the gap: 'cross-platform' in this literature always means cross-platform NON-RESPONSE, never cross-platform NON-EQUIVALENCE of the measure applied to what was donated. Asad's proposal can occupy the second meaning of the phrase, and the fact that it uses the same words as the existing work is an advantage, not a risk.
*Use in: both*

### There is a live, citable example of exactly the inference Asad wants to challenge: a 2024 Scientific Reports paper ran ONE dictionary over both news article text and social media posts and compared negativity across them.
Watson, van der Linden, Watson & Stillwell (2024), 'Negative online news articles are shared more to social media', Scientific Reports, doi 10.1038/s41598-024-71263-z. Scale reported in coverage of the paper: 95,282 articles from four US and UK news sites, against roughly 579 million Facebook and Twitter posts; negative news roughly 1.91 times more likely to be shared. Instrument: VADER, applied to full article text on the news side and to post text on the social side, with negativity reduced to a binary indicator. On my reading of the PMC full text, the authors flag dictionary methods as coarse and note corpus constraints, but do not test whether a VADER score on a full article and a VADER score on a tweet are on the same scale. This is the ideal single worked example for the proposal's opening paragraph: a good paper, in a good journal, making a cross-medium negativity claim on an instrument whose cross-medium equivalence was never established. Treat it as an illustration, not an attack.
*Use in: research proposal*

### The 'classifiers do not generalise across contexts' finding also already exists inside political communication, for topic rather than tone, and it failed. Cite it as proof the risk is real in this field, not hypothetical.
Burscher, Vliegenthart & de Vreese (2015), 'Using Supervised Machine Learning to Code Policy Issues: Can Classifiers Generalize across Contexts?', ANNALS of the AAPSS, doi 10.1177/0002716215569441. Dutch news articles and parliamentary questions, Comparative Agendas Project issue categories; cross-domain classification did not work well across those contexts. Related and worth checking directly: 'Cross-Domain Topic Classification for Political Texts', Political Analysis (Cambridge). Also relevant as agenda-setting cover: Baden, Pipal, Schoonvelde & van der Velden (2022), 'Three Gaps in Computational Text Analysis Methods for Social Sciences: A Research Agenda', Communication Methods and Measures 16(1), 1-18, doi 10.1080/19312458.2021.2015574, whose first named gap is that method development 'has prioritized technological over validity concerns'. That sentence is the best one-line justification in the literature for funding the work Asad proposes.
*Use in: research proposal*

### Framing advice that follows from all of the above: the novel unit is the CLAIM TYPE, not the finding. Everything above establishes that instruments wobble; nothing establishes what a wobbling instrument does to a between-context COMPARISON of amount.
The defensible novelty statement for the two-page proposal is narrow and should be stated in one sentence: existing work shows automated negativity measures lose ACCURACY out of domain (van Atteveldt 2021; Ribeiro 2016) and that label error BIASES downstream estimates within one setting (Egami 2023; Knox 2022; Scharkow & Bachl 2016); no work establishes whether a single negativity instrument is MEASUREMENT-EQUIVALENT across news outlets, social platforms and individual feeds, which is the precondition SP1's core research question ('how the amount and character of negativity differ across news outlets, social media platforms, and individual news diets') silently assumes. That sentence is survivable under review because every clause is attributable. What is NOT survivable: claiming to have discovered domain specificity, claiming to be first to note that machine labels bias inference, or leading with the ~25% wrong-sign figure as though it were established rather than an unpublished, unreplicated simulation on English-language UK economic policy text with no bearing yet on Danish news.
*Use in: both*

### One structural risk to name before a reviewer does: Asad's evidence is entirely English-language and the project is entirely Danish.
The advert states 'All data will be collected in Denmark.' Asad's dissertation corpus is ~279,000 English posts on UK economic policy; the unpublished manuscript with Dr Ella Haig is on the same data. The dictionary and classifier literature is explicit that language is a third axis of non-transfer alongside domain and genre: van Atteveldt et al. found English dictionaries applied to machine-translated Dutch performed as poorly as native Dutch dictionaries, and Baden et al.'s third named gap is that 'the dominance of English language tools depresses comparative research'. This cuts both ways and the proposal should use it: the fact that the Danish instrument will have to be built or revalidated from scratch is itself the argument for treating language as one more facet in the invariance design rather than a nuisance to be handled once at the start.
*Use in: motivated application*

### Team-alignment note for the motivated application: the PI's own most-cited conceptual work is on news avoidance, which is the downstream consequence of the measurement question.
Skovsgaard, M. & Andersen, K. (2019), 'Conceptualizing News Avoidance: Towards a Shared Understanding of Different Causes and Potential Solutions', Journalism Studies, doi 10.1080/1461670x.2019.1686410. The advert also names Lene Heiselberg, David Nicolas Hopmann (a comparative content-analysis specialist, which is the sub-field where invariance arguments land most naturally) and postdoc Miriam Brems. The application should connect the measurement argument to Andersen's own conceptual line: if 'amount and character of negativity' is measured non-equivalently across the outlets and platforms in a person's diet, then the diet-level exposure variable that SP2 and SP3 need for avoidance and well-being outcomes is not comparable across people whose diets are differently composed. That is a service to the project, not a critique of it, and it should be written that way.
*Use in: motivated application*
