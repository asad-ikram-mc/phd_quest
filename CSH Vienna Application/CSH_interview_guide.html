4. Your dissertation, simplyMSc Data Analytics, University of Portsmouth, 2025 · supervisor Dr Ella Haig · Distinction

In one sentence

You used AI to measure how the UK public felt about economic policies (tax, prices, welfare, energy, housing, jobs) from comments on four platforms: X, Reddit, YouTube and Quora.

Why

People react to government money decisions online first, but the reaction is spread across many sites. One combined view could warn government early. (Your Chevening idea, built small.)

What you did

  1. Collected 255,446 comments through official APIs (Reddit API, YouTube Data API v3) and licensed archives (X, Quora), with Python.
  2. Gave each a first label (positive, neutral, negative) with VADER, a rule-based sentiment word list.
  3. Fine-tuned BERT (a transformer language model) and a BiLSTM (an older neural network), compared them with logistic regression, tested with McNemar and bootstrap, and forecast daily mood with Prophet.

What it showed

  • The mood dropped sharply around the 2022 mini-budget.
  • Each platform looked different: YouTube calmer, X angrier.
  • Angry and fearful words came with negative posts (emotions from the NRC lexicon; explanations from LIME).

What you realised later

BERT looked 88% accurate, but it was trained and checked on VADER labels, the same word list it learned from, so the score measured agreement with VADER, not with people. And you had assumed “negative” means the same on every platform. That realisation became your paper.
Say this“My dissertation measured UK public mood about economic policy from 255,446 comments on four platforms. I labelled them with VADER, fine-tuned BERT on those labels and forecast mood with Prophet. BERT looked 88% accurate, but I realised it was checked against the same VADER labels it learned from, and I had assumed every platform measures the same way. That is exactly what my paper fixes.”
More detail, only if they ask
Models: BERT 88% vs BiLSTM 82% vs simple baselines, all measured against the word-list (VADER) labels. Tests: McNemar and bootstrap; forecasting with Prophet; emotions with the NRC word list. A few numbers in the text didn't match the code records (for example 279,000 vs 255,446 comments); the paper lists these openly. Ethics approved as low risk.

5. Your paper, simply“Is the Platform Part of the Measurement?” · Ikram & Haig · arXiv 2610.16757 · October 2026 · your first paper (a preprint)

In one sentence

Before you add up AI-measured opinions from different social media sites, check that each site is being measured the same way, because often it isn't.

The problem, as a picture

The simple idea

Surveys know people answer differently on the phone and on paper, so they check before comparing. Your paper says each social media site is like a different “way of asking”, so check it the same way.

What you did

Wrote a 6-step protocol using tools from survey research (generalizability theory, measurement invariance and DIF, score linking) to test and fix the comparison, and ran a simulation in Python (500 runs) where the true mood was known, to show what goes wrong if you skip it.

The 6 steps (tap through)

How your simulation works

A simulation is a computer experiment: you invent data where you already know the true answer, then check whether a method finds it. You wrote it in Python (NumPy), and the code is public on GitHub (asadfix/platform-measurement).

  • What “bias, noise, cut-offs” mean: on one site people sound harsher (bias), posts vary (noise), and the AI draws its positive/negative lines in different places (cut-offs). Same true mood, different labels.
  • Result: the naive index pointed the wrong way on 13.8 of 60 days; the adjusted index on 0.8. The more the sites differ, the worse the naive index gets.
  • Honest note: in the simulation you know each site’s bias; with real data it must be estimated, which is what steps 1–5 do.

7. Artemis AI, simplyCo-founder and CTO · team of eight · beta on 15 Dec 2026

In one sentence

Artemis AI helps creators and businesses understand their social media pages: what people are saying, what is working, when a wave of hostile comments starts, and which pages they could collaborate with.

The data

20 consenting Facebook pages; 111,000+ posts and 3M+ comments, plus images and videos, collected through the Meta Graph API into an S3 / Apache Iceberg data lake queried with Athena. Images read with CLIP and OCR, video with Whisper.

The AI

Claude labels 6,000 posts and explains why; rules from those reasons guide DeepSeek, which labels 12,000 more; your own models (XLM-R, DistilBERT, DeBERTa) learn from both; people check samples. 48 models are planned for the beta, plus Art-E, an advisor on Amazon Bedrock.

The network

An early map of how pages relate (an affinity graph), grouping similar pages with Louvain community detection, to suggest good collaborations with a match score. Mostly still being built.

The page network, as a picture

Dots = pages. Each colour is a community: pages whose audiences and content look alike, found by Louvain community detection.
Solid lines = strong similarity (affinity) inside a community; dashed grey = weak bridges between communities.
Gold line = a suggested collaboration. The goal is a partner that opens a new audience, not a lookalike, so good matches often cross communities.

How the network is built

Page data from the warehouse→Signals: topics, content types, image fingerprints (CLIP), formats, posting times→Similarity between every pair of pages→Affinity graph→Louvain communities→Match score 0–100, top 5 partners

Illustrative picture, not real pages. Only consenting pilot pages and page-level data are used; Meta does not give commenter identity. Status: an early prototype; communities and the match score are being built. At CSH you would learn the network science to do this properly (diffusion, bias in networks).

What it taught you (the research lessons)

Agreement can lie

Two AI models (Claude and DeepSeek) agreed more while both were getting worse. So you check AI against people, not against other AI.

Labels drift

One measure fell from 77% to 37% in weeks without anyone noticing. So you monitor labels all the time.

“Normal” is hard

Out of 207,192 comments on busy posts, almost none were real hostile storms. Every page needs its own baseline.

Words depend on the group

Banter, dialect and emoji (like 😂) get mistaken for abuse or flip labels, so you built filters and a language check.
Say this“At Artemis AI, my start-up, we label millions of comments with AI. I learned that labels can look reliable exactly when they're failing, and that every page needs its own baseline. We're building a human-checked test set now. Measuring whether AI labels are right is exactly what I want to study at CSH, and I'd like to learn network science to do the page network properly.”
More detail: architecture and all 48 models (only if they ask)

Status: 2 of 35 product models finished, 11 in progress. Say “48 models in the agreed architecture, being built for the beta”. Honest point: no client-facing model has a human-checked accuracy figure yet.

Network ideas from your CEO (not built): identity gravity, signal economy, diffusion paths. Two research catches: Meta doesn't give commenter identity, so matching uses page-level data only; and you had to define a “good match” (new audience) before scoring one.
