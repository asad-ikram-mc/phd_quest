4. Your dissertation, simplyMSc Data Analytics, University of Portsmouth, 2025 · supervisor Dr Ella Haig · Distinction

In one sentence

You used AI to measure how the UK public felt about economic policies (tax, prices, welfare, energy, housing, jobs) from comments on four platforms: X, Reddit, YouTube and Quora.

Why

People react to government money decisions online first, but the reaction is spread across many sites. One combined view could warn government early. (Your Chevening idea, built small.)

What you did

  1. Collected 255,446 comments through official APIs (Reddit API, YouTube Data API v3) and licensed archives (X, Quora), with Python.
  2. Gave each a first label (positive, neutral, negative) with VADER, a rule-based sentiment word list.
  3. Fine-tuned BERT (a transformer language model) and a BiLSTM (an older neural network), compared them with logistic regression, tested with McNemar and bootstrap, and forecast daily mood with Prophet.

What it showed

  • The mood dropped sharply around the 2022 mini-budget.
  • Each platform looked different: YouTube calmer, X angrier.
  • Angry and fearful words came with negative posts (emotions from the NRC lexicon; explanations from LIME).

What you realised later

BERT looked 88% accurate, but it was trained and checked on VADER labels, the same word list it learned from, so the score measured agreement with VADER, not with people. And you had assumed “negative” means the same on every platform. That realisation became your paper.
Say this“My dissertation measured UK public mood about economic policy from 255,446 comments on four platforms. I labelled them with VADER, fine-tuned BERT on those labels and forecast mood with Prophet. BERT looked 88% accurate, but I realised it was checked against the same VADER labels it learned from, and I had assumed every platform measures the same way. That is exactly what my paper fixes.”
More detail, only if they ask
  • Scores: BERT 88% (macro-F1 0.878), BiLSTM 82%, logistic regression 71%, VADER 65%. All measured against the VADER labels, which is the problem.
  • Hardest label: “neutral”, often confused with “negative”.
  • Tests: McNemar (do two models make different mistakes?) and bootstrap (how sure are we?); chi-square (does mood differ by topic?).
  • Confidence: temperature scaling, so “90% sure” means right 90% of the time.
  • Forecasting: Prophet, trained to May 2025, tested on June 2025.
  • Emotions and explanations: NRC word list for emotions; LIME and integrated gradients show which words drove a decision.
  • Records: some numbers in the text don’t match the code (279,000 vs 255,446 comments). Your paper lists them openly. Always say 255,446.
  • Ethics: approved by Portsmouth as low risk (TETHIC-2025-111094).

5. Your paper, simply“Is the Platform Part of the Measurement?” · Ikram & Haig · arXiv 2610.16757 · October 2026 · your first paper (a preprint)

In one sentence

Before you add up AI-measured opinions from different social media sites, check that each site is being measured the same way, because often it isn't.

The problem, as a picture

The simple idea

Surveys know people answer differently on the phone and on paper, so they check before comparing. Your paper says each social media site is like a different “way of asking”, so check it the same way.

What you did

Wrote a 6-step protocol using tools from survey research (generalizability theory, measurement invariance and DIF, score linking) to test and fix the comparison, and ran a simulation in Python (500 runs) where the true mood was known, to show what goes wrong if you skip it.

The 6 steps (tap through)

How your simulation works

A simulation is a computer experiment: you invent data where you already know the true answer, then check whether a method finds it. You wrote it in Python (NumPy), and the code is public on GitHub (asadfix/platform-measurement).

  • What “bias, noise, cut-offs” mean: on one site people sound harsher (bias), posts vary (noise), and the AI draws its positive/negative lines in different places (cut-offs). Same true mood, different labels.
  • Result: the naive index pointed the wrong way on 13.8 of 60 days; the adjusted index on 0.8. The more the sites differ, the worse the naive index gets.
  • Honest note: in the simulation you know each site’s bias; with real data it must be estimated, which is what steps 1–5 do.

Answer 1 · A research project, and what went wrong

In short: your MSc dissertation, and the mistake you found in it.

  • What: UK public reaction to economic policy, from 255,446 comments on X, Reddit, YouTube and Quora (official APIs and licensed archives), ethics approved.
  • How: first labels from VADER; compared BiLSTM and a fine-tuned BERT; tested with McNemar and bootstrap, chi-square across topics; confidence fixed with calibration; forecasts with Prophet; emotions with NRC; explanations with LIME and integrated gradients; 200 posts checked by hand.
  • What went wrong: BERT scored 0.878 macro-F1, but against VADER-seeded labels; your 200-post check reviewed VADER’s labels, so the kappa of 0.78 measured agreement with VADER, not between people. And you’d assumed “negative” means the same on every platform.
  • What you learned: a score describes its yardstick as much as the model, so first ask where the yardstick comes from; and test that a measure works the same in each group before comparing (a normal step in survey research).
  • What came of it: the paper with Dr Haig, treating each platform as a survey mode; for the real-data study you fixed 15 policy events in advance.
Answer 2 · A discovery from a CSH group

The paper: Chen, Kmetty, Iñiguez & Omodei (2025), “The public that engages invisibly”, Communication Methods and Measures 19(4).

  • Their finding, in one line: by linking a survey of Hungarian internet users to the YouTube histories they donated (data donation), they show people who comment on political videos are more polarised than people who only watch, so comment-based studies overstate division.
  • Your question: is that commenter–watcher gap stable across platforms, topics and time? If so, a small donated panel could correct polarisation estimates from millions of public comments.
  • Why it interests you: same shape as your problem. Comments are a big, cheap, biased signal; donated data is small, costly and closer to the truth, just as a small hand-labelled sample anchors a machine-labelled corpus (score linking). If the gap isn’t stable, knowing where it breaks is itself a finding.
  • Your link: brands at Fluency and Artemis AI judge pages by comments, though most of their audience never comments.
Answer 3 · A problem you want to study

The question: when can a machine-labelled measure (sentiment, stance or a narrative frame) be compared across platforms, AI models and time, and how do we correct it before tracking how narratives spread?

  • Why it matters: research pools platforms and uses LLMs as labellers, so a difference can be real change, a platform effect, or the model (which changes version). Papers you cited: Gaynor et al. 2026 (legislators look different across votes, speeches and tweets); Fan et al. 2025 (removing the medium from embeddings with concept erasure); Licht et al. 2025 (LLMs measuring scales); Hoyle et al. 2021 (automated metrics drifting from human judgement).
  • Why it’s doable, in four steps: (1) extend your simulation with models and time as factors, no new data needed; (2) collect posts around fixed events through official APIs, labelled by several LLMs, with a larger hand-labelled sample per platform and a second coder; then a G-study and DIF tests; (3) concept erasure to test whether removing the platform signal removes real differences; (4) put corrected and uncorrected measures into a network diffusion analysis.
  • Who it helps: researchers who pool platforms or switch AI models; public bodies, NGOs and journalists reading social-listening dashboards; anyone asking if a narrative really moved between platforms.
  • What you’d learn at CSH: the network science of diffusion.
