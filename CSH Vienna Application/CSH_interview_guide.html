4. Your dissertation, simplyMSc Data Analytics, University of Portsmouth, 2025 · supervisor Dr Ella Haig · Distinction

In one sentence

You used AI to measure how the UK public felt about economic policies (tax, prices, welfare, energy, housing, jobs) from comments on four platforms: X, Reddit, YouTube and Quora.

Why

People react to government money decisions online first, but the reaction is spread across many sites. One combined view could warn government early. (Your Chevening idea, built small.)

What you did

  1. Collected 255,446 comments.
  2. Gave each a first label (positive, neutral, negative) with a simple word list.
  3. Trained AI models to label them, then tracked the mood over time.

What it showed

  • The mood dropped sharply around the 2022 mini-budget.
  • Each platform looked different: YouTube calmer, X angrier.
  • Angry and fearful words came with negative posts.

What you realised later

The AI looked 88% accurate, but it was checked against labels from the same word list it learned from, so the score proved little. And you had assumed “negative” means the same on every platform. That realisation became your paper.
Say this“My dissertation measured UK public mood about economic policy from 255,446 comments on four platforms. The model looked accurate, but I realised I had checked it against labels from the same word list, and assumed every platform measures the same way. That is exactly what my paper fixes.”
More detail, only if they ask
Models: BERT 88% vs BiLSTM 82% vs simple baselines, all measured against the word-list (VADER) labels. Tests: McNemar and bootstrap; forecasting with Prophet; emotions with the NRC word list. A few numbers in the text didn't match the code records (for example 279,000 vs 255,446 comments); the paper lists these openly. Ethics approved as low risk.

5. Your paper, simply“Is the Platform Part of the Measurement?” · Ikram & Haig · arXiv 2610.16757 · October 2026 · your first paper (a preprint)

In one sentence

Before you add up AI-measured opinions from different social media sites, check that each site is being measured the same way, because often it isn't.

The problem, as a picture

The simple idea

Surveys know people answer differently on the phone and on paper, so they check before comparing. Your paper says each social media site is like a different “way of asking”, so check it the same way.

What you did

Wrote a 6-step checklist to test and fix the comparison, and ran a computer experiment where the true mood was known, to show what goes wrong if you skip it.

The 6 steps (tap through)

The result

Just add sites together
~14 of 60 days wrong
Check and fix first
under 1 day

Days when the overall mood pointed the wrong way (happy when people were unhappy, or the reverse).

Say this (20 seconds)“People write differently on Reddit than on YouTube, so AI reads their opinions differently. Adding sites together can flip the answer: in my experiment the overall mood pointed the wrong way on nearly a quarter of days. My paper gives a checklist to test whether a comparison is fair, and a way to fix it.”
More detail, only if they ask
  • Your part: you built the computer experiment and the dissertation example; written with Dr Haig.
  • The gap: researchers knew platforms shape how people write (Sen et al. 2021 call it “platform-affordance error”) but nobody had a test for it.
  • Technical names of the steps: same-event comparison, reliability study, measurement invariance and DIF, multilevel adjustment, score linking, simulation.
  • Fairness built in: 15 UK budget events (centred on the 2022 mini-budget) were fixed before looking at data; every test is repeated with a second AI model.
  • Limits: it's a method plus an experiment; real data on all four platforms is the next study. UK and English only.
  • Timeline: idea from the MSc → written with Dr Haig → live on arXiv 14 Oct 2026.

The system, end to end

The 48 models, by group (tap a group)

Status today: 2 of 35 product models finished, 11 in progress; the rest planned. Say “48 models in the agreed architecture, being built for the beta”.

The labelling chain

Five tasks on every post: sentiment, emotion, topic, intent, toxicity. Claude labels cost about $10 for 6,000; DeepSeek about $2.50 for 12,000. Next: a blind, stratified human gold set with a second labeller.

Network theory: built, planned and vision

Built (early)

A first page network from warehouse data only (topics, content types, image fingerprints, formats, posting times), for consenting pilot pages, with no personal data. A page-to-page affinity graph links pages whose audiences comment alike; a prototype runs Louvain community detection (finds clusters in a graph) and a 0–100 match score.

Planned

Audience communities in the page graph; a collaboration match score rated on 100 page pairs; “new-group reach” (which partner opens a new audience); a collab outcome estimator (did a collab lift both pages over 30 days?); opt-in weekly Discovery; a rising-topics tool tested against a seasonal baseline, with groups under 5 pages hidden.

Vision (strategy, not built)

Your CEO's ideas: identity gravity (creators whose content mix drifts together may suit a collab), a signal economy (different weights for likes, comments, shares, collabs) and diffusion paths (ideas spreading cluster → bridge → cluster), always as probabilities.
Two research catches you can mention“Our Discovery plan assumed Meta gives commenter identity; it doesn't, so we rebuilt it on page-level signals only. And we had to define a good match before scoring one: similarity favours lookalike pages, but the goal is reaching a new audience. Both are exactly the measurement and network questions I want to study at CSH.”

Challenges: gap, what you found, what you did

Gap
Found
Done
The agreement trap
Agreement between two models rose on 3 of 5 tasks while both collapsed onto the most common answer.
Regression checks use fixed human-checked labels, never another model.
Silent drift
Post-sentiment agreement fell from 77% to 37% after mid-September; emoji-heavy posts flipped positive labels.
Drift monitoring, a text-drift alarm, a shared vocabulary for toxicity.
What is a storm?
207,192 comments on 369 busy posts (Feb–Jul): no real storms, one comment aimed at the page.
Busy-but-normal hours became a false-alarm test set; storms need several signals to agree, against each page's normal.
What is normal?
Engagement across all 20 pages fell ~97% in late July: a platform change, not content.
An era detector and a metric registry recording when Meta changed definitions.
Banter and dialect
Labellers missed hostility people saw (2% vs 15% negative), and read banter as abuse.
Banter/dialect filter and a language check that lets the model abstain.
Reposts
A second poster of the same caption got ~31% less, but pages repost winners and matching was text-only.
Image-hash repost check; show “your 3 most similar past posts did…” instead of predicting.
Predicting flops
The flop model was barely above chance (55% pairwise), below published work.
Compare each post only with the same page's earlier posts; keep a page-condition warning, drop single-draft scores.
Platform dependence
Meta controls access through app review; commenter identity isn't available.
Ask for permissions step by step; design around page-level data.
