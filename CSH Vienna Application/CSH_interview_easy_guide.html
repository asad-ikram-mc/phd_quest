3. Your paper, explained simply

Title: “Is the Platform Part of the Measurement? A Protocol and Simulation Study for Cross-Platform Equivalence of Machine-Labelled Policy Sentiment”. Ikram and Haig, arXiv 2610.16757, October 2026. Your first paper; a preprint, not yet peer reviewed.

The problem in one breath

Researchers and dashboards often collect posts from several platforms, let an AI score each post as positive, neutral or negative, and add everything up. But people write differently on each platform, so the AI may score the same feeling differently on each one. Then the total can be wrong.

The idea: treat a platform like a survey “mode”

Survey researchers know that people answer the same question differently by phone and on paper, and they test for this before comparing. Your paper says platforms are like modes too, so test them the same way.

What the paper does, in plain words

  • Step 1, same-event check: compare platforms on the same news moment. You fixed 15 UK budget and economic events in advance (centred on the September 2022 mini-budget), before looking at any counts, so you couldn’t pick events that suit the answer.
  • Step 2, reliability check: how much of the score comes from the platform, the AI model or the event, compared against posts labelled by people.
  • Step 3, “same meaning” test: does “negative” behave the same on Reddit, X, YouTube and Quora? (Technical names: measurement invariance and DIF.)
  • Step 4, adjust for the crowd and timing: different people post on each site at different times.
  • Step 5, one common scale: convert every platform to one yardstick, like converting currencies before adding.
  • Step 6, a simulation: a computer experiment where you know the true mood, to show what goes wrong if you skip steps 1 to 5.

The headline result

Over 60 simulated days, simply adding platforms together showed the mood pointing the wrong way on about 14 days (nearly a quarter). Correcting each platform first: under 1 day. Repeated 500 times, so it is not luck.

Where it came from

The paper uses your dissertation as its real example: 255,446 comments on 6,650 posts about six UK economic topics (tax, cost of living, welfare, energy, housing, jobs). It openly reports that your model was checked against labels from the same word list it learned from, and that some dissertation numbers don’t match the records. Being open about this is a strength: it shows research honesty.

Its honest limits (know these)

  • People choose their platforms, so differences mix “who posts” with “how the platform shapes posts”.
  • Four platforms, UK only, English only.
  • It measures expressed feeling, not support for a policy: a negative post about inflation isn’t opposition to a policy.
  • It is a method plus a simulation; the full real-data study comes next, with 1,000–1,500 comments labelled by people and a second annotator.

4. Your dissertation, explained simply

“Cross-Platform Sentiment Analysis of Public Reaction to UK Economic Policies” · MSc Data Analytics, University of Portsmouth, September 2025 · supervisor Dr Ella Haig · Distinction (project mark 78/100) · ethics approved as low risk.

What it tried to do

Build the Chevening idea at small scale: collect what the UK public said online about economic policy on four platforms, measure the mood with AI, and see if past mood can forecast the next weeks.

Its four questions, in plain words

  • Can modern AI read public mood about economic policy?
  • How accurate are two AI models (BERT and BiLSTM)?
  • Can past mood forecast short-term reactions?
  • How does mood differ by topic and over time?

What you did

  • Data: public comments from X/Twitter, Reddit, YouTube and Quora through official APIs and licensed archives. Use 255,446 comments, the number the records confirm.
  • Labelling: a word-list tool (VADER) gave first labels; then you trained BERT (a modern language model) and a BiLSTM (an older neural model) and compared them with simple baselines.
  • Checks: statistical tests (McNemar, bootstrap), calibration (are the model’s confidence levels honest?), and LIME to show which words drove a decision.
  • Extras: emotion analysis (anger and fear went with negative posts) and Prophet forecasting of daily mood.

What it reported

  • BERT matched the labels 88% of the time, ahead of the BiLSTM (82%) and simple baselines; “neutral” was the hardest class.
  • The mood index dipped sharply around the September 2022 mini-budget, and forecasts beat simple baselines.
  • Platforms differed: YouTube looked more positive (explanatory videos), X had sharper negative spikes, Quora was sparse and noisy, and X dominated the data.

What you found later (the honest part)

“The 88% was agreement with labels that came from the same word list, so it proved less than it seemed. When I rechecked my records for the paper, a few numbers in the text also didn’t match. I report all of that openly in the paper, and it is exactly why the paper exists.”

If asked about topic results, don’t defend exact percentages; say the labels need checking against people first, which is what the follow-up study does.

12. Glossary: hard words, made simple

TermMeaning
Sentiment analysisUsing a computer to judge if a text is positive, neutral or negative.
VADERA simple tool that scores sentiment by looking words up in a list.
BERT, BiLSTMAI models that learn to read text; BERT is newer and usually better.
LLM (Claude, DeepSeek)Large language models, here used to label posts instead of people.
Accuracy, F1How often a model’s labels match a reference. Only meaningful if the reference is trustworthy.
Circular validationChecking a model against labels from the same source it learned from. It proves little.
Measurement invarianceDoes a measure mean the same thing in different groups (here, platforms)?
DIF“Differential item functioning”: one label, like “negative”, behaving differently in one group.
Generalizability studySplits a score’s variation into its sources: platform, model, event, people.
Score linkingPutting different scales onto one yardstick, like converting currencies.
Simulation with ground truthA computer experiment where you set the true answer, so you can see how a method goes wrong.
Anchor eventsFixed news moments used to compare platforms on the same thing at the same time.
Data donationPeople voluntarily share their own platform data (e.g. YouTube history) with researchers.
PolarisationGroups moving apart; affective polarisation means disliking the other side.
Network diffusionHow ideas or narratives spread through connections between people or platforms.
Concept erasureRemoving one piece of information (such as which platform) from a model’s view of a text.
Preprint / arXivA public research paper shared before peer review.
