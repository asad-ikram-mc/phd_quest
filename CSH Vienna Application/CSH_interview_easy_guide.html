2. your story, step by step

1. Dubizzle Labs (2022–2024)

Built the company’s first web data system: 15 markets, four languages, a team of three. Dashboards showed what customers said online. For the PhD: you know how the same opinion reads differently across languages.

2. Chevening (2024)

Won for helping students into remote careers, and for an idea: one system comparing social media platforms to give government evidence of public opinion. For the PhD: your question is years old and useful to the public, not a trend.

3. MSc dissertation (2025)

Built the Chevening idea small: 255,446 comments from X, Reddit, YouTube and Quora about UK economic policy. Then you found the check on your AI model was circular. For the PhD: you can find the flaw in your own work and turn it into research.

4. M+C Saatchi Fluency (2024–now)

Social media from ten platforms every day, for big brands and UK government. You record which prompt produced every AI label, and built AI agents that fix broken data collectors. For the PhD: you can build clean, repeatable datasets from day one.

5. Artemis AI (2025–now)

Your start-up: 3 million+ comments, 48 models, a team of eight. The hard lessons:

  • Agreement can lie: two AI models agreed more while both got worse. Always check against people.
  • Labels drift: one measure fell from 77% agreement to 37% in weeks without anyone noticing.
  • “Normal” is hard: out of 207,192 comments on busy posts, almost none were real storms. Each page needs its own baseline.
  • Words depend on the group: banter and dialect get mistaken for abuse.

For the PhD: checking AI labels against people is exactly what CSH’s Alexander Hoyle works on.

6. Your first paper (October 2026)

All of this became one method: test whether platforms can be compared, and fix them if not. For the PhD: it is chapter one, already done.

4. what you told CSH in your application

The interview recording is read together with these answers, so say the same things.

Answer 1: your project

Your MSc dissertation compared reactions to UK economic policy on four platforms. The AI model looked very accurate, but it had been trained and checked on labels from the same simple word list (VADER), so the check proved nothing. You also assumed “negative” means the same on every site. Lesson: always ask where your yardstick comes from.

Answer 2: a CSH paper you liked

Chen, Kmetty, Iñiguez and Omodei (2025): people who comment on political YouTube videos are more polarised than people who only watch. So studies built on comments make society look more divided than it is. Your idea: could a small group of people who donate their data be used to correct millions of public comments?

Answer 3: what you want to study

When can we compare AI-labelled opinions across platforms, AI models and time, and how do we fix them when we can’t? Four steps: extend your simulation; collect new posts around fixed events, labelled by several AIs and checked by people; test whether removing the “platform signal” removes real differences; then see how the fix changes how narratives seem to spread.

People and projects you named (all checked)

  • Alexander Hoyle, CSH faculty: checking whether AI text measures really measure what they claim. Closest to you.
  • Elisa Omodei, CSH faculty: co-author of the paper in Answer 2.
  • Springboard projects: Understanding narratives at scale; Polarization and minority representation on online platforms.

6. your paper in 20 seconds

“People write differently on Reddit than on YouTube, so AI reads their opinions differently. Adding platforms together can flip the answer: in my simulation, the overall mood pointed the wrong way on nearly a quarter of days. My paper gives a checklist to test whether a comparison is fair, and a way to fix it.”
