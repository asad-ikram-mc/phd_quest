2. Research

“Describe a research project and your role.”

My dissertation: I collected about 255,000 comments under ethics approval, fine-tuned BERT against BiLSTM and lexicon baselines, and tested the differences. The model looked very good, but I realised its labels had come from the VADER lexicon, so the check was circular. I’d also assumed “negative” means the same on Reddit as on YouTube. With Dr Haig I turned that into a paper that treats each platform like a survey mode, the way survey researchers handle phone versus paper answers. I built the simulation: if you just add platforms together, the overall mood points the wrong way on nearly a quarter of days; correct each platform first and it drops to under one day in sixty.

“What went wrong, and what did you learn?”

That an agreement score describes the reference as much as the model. Now my first question is where the yardstick comes from and whether it’s independent. At Artemis AI we check our labels against posts the team labelled by hand.

“Explain your paper simply.” (have the 20-second version ready)

AI reads opinions differently on Reddit than on YouTube, because people write differently on each. So adding platforms together can flip the answer. My paper gives a checklist to test whether a comparison is fair, and a way to correct it when it isn’t.

3. Industry experience, told as research

“What have you built, and what did it teach you?”

  • Fluency: analyst on a twelve-month study of about 270,000 Brandwatch and Reddit mentions, with a topic codebook and LLM tagging where every tag records its prompt version, piloted three times before the full run. I also built AI agents that repair and create our collectors, with rules and approval gates.
  • Artemis AI: Claude labels posts and explains each label; rules mined from those explanations guide a cheaper model, whose labels train our own classifiers. We built a detector for sudden storms of hostile comments and filters so dialect and banter aren’t read as toxicity, which is a measurement problem in miniature.
  • Lesson: the numbers that brands and governments act on are rarely checked against people. That’s why I want a PhD.

4. Motivation and fit

“Why a PhD, and why now?”

Industry taught me to collect and label online data at scale; it didn’t teach me to show rigorously what those numbers say about people. Publishing the paper showed me this is the work I enjoy most, and I want the training to do it properly, then carry it back into how these tools are built.

“Why CSH, and why complexity science?”

Because opinion online is a system, not a pile of posts: people, platforms and algorithms shape each other, and what we can see is a selected slice. Chen, Kmetty, Iñiguez and Omodei showed that commenters are more polarised than people who only watch, which is my problem in another form. Alexander Hoyle’s work on validating NLP measures is the closest to mine, and the narratives-at-scale and online polarisation projects are where my question goes next. I also want to learn network science and diffusion, which I don’t have yet.

5. Your PhD idea

“What would you like to work on?”

Before we compare a narrative or polarisation measure across platforms, LLM annotators or time, test whether the comparison is fair, and correct it when it isn’t. First, extend my simulation so models and time are factors alongside platforms. Second, build a corpus around events fixed in advance, labelled by several LLMs with a hand-labelled sample per platform. Third, put corrected and uncorrected measures into a diffusion analysis and show when conclusions change.

“Why is it doable?”

The statistical tools are mature, the simulation already runs, and collecting data at scale has been my job for eight years. What’s new is treating platforms and models as the groups being compared.
