3. What you did at Fluency and Artemis AI

M+C Saatchi Fluency (London, since December 2024). Fluency is the social and web intelligence side of M+C Saatchi, and its reports go to global brands such as Amazon, Ford, Nike and Reckitt, and to UK government communications teams. Your work there has two halves.

  • Research and analysis. You were an analyst on a twelve-month study of public discourse: 100,845 Brandwatch mentions and 170,542 Reddit mentions. Instead of hand-reading them, the team built a codebook from BERTopic topics, then used an LLM to tag every mention. The study used two research habits you now treat as standard: every tag carries a hash of the exact prompt version that produced it, so results can be traced and repeated; and the prompt was run three times on the same 500 rows before the full run, to see whether the labels were stable. In your portfolio you called this treating a prompt as an instrument whose version must be recorded. It is the closest you have come to studying narratives at scale.
  • Data and AI engineering. You collect from ten platforms every day, feeding a governed master table. When a forum changes its layout, a crawler usually breaks silently: the series does not stop, it just thins, which quietly biases any trend. You designed a self-healing AI agent that diagnoses the break, rewrites the collector, and tests it on the live site before anything ships, plus a builder agent that creates new collectors. Both follow fixed rules, and a person approves every change.

Artemis AI (co-founder and CTO, since 2025). A social media intelligence start-up helping creators and businesses understand their audiences, heading to a beta in December 2026. You lead the technology in a team of eight.

  • Data. More than 3 million Facebook and Instagram comments on 111,000+ posts, collected through Meta’s official API, with images and videos read through OCR, CLIP and Whisper.
  • Labelling, which is your research question inside a product. Claude labels about 6,000 posts and writes a reason for each label. The team hand-labelled about 500 posts and comments to check both models, and you are building evaluation around a human-labelled golden set so no model reaches users unchecked.

4. What you told CSH (keep answers consistent)

Answer 1: a research project, your role, and what did not go as expected. Your MSc dissertation at Portsmouth (2025, supervisor Dr Ella Haig) asked how public reaction to UK economic policy differed across platforms. It was yours from question to write-up: you obtained ethics approval, collected 255,446 public comments from X, Reddit, YouTube and Quora through official APIs and licensed feeds, and built the whole analysis in Python: VADER and BiLSTM baselines, a fine-tuned BERT classifier, calibration, McNemar and bootstrap tests, chi-square tests across topics, Prophet forecasts, NRC emotions, and LIME and integrated gradients to see what the model relied on. You also hand-labelled 200 posts.

What went wrong, told honestly: BERT scored a macro-F1 of 0.878, but its training labels had been seeded by the VADER lexicon, the score was largely in-sample, and your 200-post check reviewed VADER’s own labels, so the agreement (kappa about 0.78) measured agreement with VADER, not truth. The validation was circular: it showed BERT had learned to copy VADER. Underneath was a second untested assumption, that “negative” means the same on Reddit as on YouTube. What you learned: an agreement score describes the reference as much as the model, so the first design question is where the yardstick comes from; and a comparison between groups needs evidence that the instrument behaves alike in each, a routine test in survey research that text analysis rarely runs. That became the paper with Dr Haig, and for its application you fixed fifteen policy events in advance, before computing any counts.

Answer 2: a discovery from a CSH group. You chose Chen, Kmetty, Iñiguez and Omodei (2025), “The public that engages invisibly”, Communication Methods and Measures 19(4): 294–312. By linking a survey of Hungarian internet users to the YouTube histories they donated, they show that people who comment on political videos are more polarised and more selective than people who only watch, so studies built on visible traces such as comments overstate how divided audiences are. Your question: is the gap between commenters and watchers stable enough, across platforms, topics and time, that a small donated panel could correct polarisation estimates built from public comments at scale? Why it matters to you: it has the same structure as your problem. Public comments are a large, cheap, selected instrument; donated data is a small, expensive, closer criterion. If the relation holds steady, a few hundred donors could calibrate millions of comments, just as a small hand-labelled sample anchors a machine-labelled corpus; if it breaks, knowing where is itself a finding. You meet the same gap at work: brands judge their pages by comments, though most of their audience never comments.

Answer 3: a problem you want to explore. Statement: when is a machine-labelled construct (sentiment, stance or a narrative frame) comparable across platforms, annotator models and time, and how should the differences be corrected before we track how narratives spread between platforms? Why it matters: research on narratives and polarisation increasingly pools platforms and uses LLMs as annotators, and any difference mixes real change with how each platform elicits expression and how each model reads it, while the models change version underneath us. You cited Gaynor, Miler, Goel, Hoyle and Resnik (2026, Journal of Politics): legislators’ positions differ across votes, speeches and tweets; Fan et al. (2025) on removing the medium from embeddings by concept erasure; Licht et al. (2025) on LLMs measuring scalar constructs; and Hoyle et al. (2021) on automated metrics drifting from human judgement. Why tractable, in four steps: (1) your simulation has known ground truth and runs in seconds, so you can add models and time as facets with no new data; (2) you can build an event-anchored corpus from official APIs, labelled by several LLMs, with a larger hand-labelled sample per platform and a second coder, then split the variance and test each frame for DIF; (3) concept erasure tests whether removing the platform signal also removes real differences; (4) corrected and uncorrected measures go into a network diffusion analysis to show when conclusions change. For whom: researchers who pool platforms or switch models mid-study; public bodies, NGOs and journalists reading social-listening dashboards; anyone asking whether a narrative really moved between platforms. What you would learn at CSH: the network science of diffusion.

6. Your paper in 20 seconds

“AI reads opinions differently on Reddit than on YouTube, because people write differently on each. So adding platforms together can flip the answer: in my simulation, the overall mood points the wrong way on nearly a quarter of days, and under one day in sixty once each platform is corrected. The paper gives a checklist to test whether a comparison is fair, and a way to fix it.”
