## The research direction: accountable instruments for NLP measurement of public opinion

Computational social science and the industries around it now measure public opinion by having NLP systems, increasingly LLMs, read social media at scale, pooled across platforms and tracked over years. Three properties of that instrument are untested. Nobody checks whether its scores mean the same thing on every platform it reads. Nobody corrects a measurement series when the underlying model is updated, although proprietary models change under silent backend updates and the evidence shows reasonable configuration choices alone can flip the statistical conclusions of published annotation studies about a third of the time. And nobody quantifies how much of a measurement depends on the standpoint built into the model, even though LLMs demonstrably encode political and cultural positions. Between April and August 2026 the field converged on this diagnosis in a run of papers arguing that annotation is measurement, that model-prompt regimes disagree with each other, and that persona conditioning shifts labels along ideological lines. What nobody has built is the calibration discipline, and that is the doctorate I am proposing.

The foundation exists. My methods paper with Dr Ella Haig (arXiv, 2026) treats each platform as a survey mode and specifies a six-step psychometric protocol, generalizability theory, measurement invariance and differential item functioning, and score linking, for machine-labelled sentiment, with a 500-replication known-truth simulation showing that naive pooling reports the wrong sign of aggregate sentiment on a quarter of days. The doctoral work carries that machinery from fitted classifiers to LLM instruments in three strands, each sized to a thesis chapter and a paper. First, platform equivalence: fitted categorical invariance and DIF models on LLM-scored opinion constructs with platform as the grouping variable and a human-coded criterion inside the measurement model, which no study has yet done; the nearest work builds equivalence frameworks for language rather than platform, or validates portable measures by correlation, which cannot detect the scalar non-invariance that flips pooled trends. Second, instrument change: a shadow-anchored bridging design, a pinned open-weight annotator no vendor can retire, an executed version ladder, and stratified misclassification corrections, so a longitudinal series stays comparable across model updates, silent ones included; existing work detects change and decides whether a swap is safe, but nothing corrects the series when it is not. Third, standpoint: a multivariate generalizability analysis of how the epistemic configuration of the instrument moves the measurement, and whether those effects travel across platforms. A criterion test runs through all three: with selection correction held fixed, does a calibrated multi-platform index track probability-sample surveys better than a naive pooled one? Either answer is publishable.

## Why this is a responsible NLP project, theme by theme

This is not an application about social media that happens to mention responsibility. Each of the CDT's skill domains is load-bearing in the design.

Responsible NLP data and models. The project's core object is bias in the measurement sense: labels that systematically mean different things across platforms, model versions and standpoints. The human criterion sample is built the way the CDT teaches data should be built, with a pre-registered codebook, double coding, paid annotators, and an inauthenticity screen so the pipeline never scores bot or machine-written text as public opinion.

Explainable NLP for users in-the-world. The project's flagship deliverable is an Instrument Change Log, a one-page reporting standard for annotation pipelines, the model card's equivalent for measurement, plus a pip-installable toolkit that logs model, prompt hash, version and seed for every label. The users in the world are concrete: media observatories, regulators exercising platform data access, and analysts like the ones I have served for seven years, none of whom can currently explain why their trend line moved.

NLP governance and accountability. Platform measurement is now a legal act. DSA systemic-risk assessments, Article 40 researcher access, and Ofcom's Online Safety Act duties all consume exactly the kind of scores this project calibrates, and civil-society reviews of the first DSA risk assessments already complain that the measurement is weak. A defensible calibration standard is governance infrastructure, and I want to build it with the CDT's grounding in the regulatory landscape rather than guessing at it from the engineering side.

Designing for human-NLP partnership. The protocol is human-in-the-loop by construction: human coders anchor the measurement model rather than serving as a one-off validation, and the gold-set refresh policy makes the human role a standing part of the instrument, not scaffolding to be discarded.

## Why Edinburgh, and why these supervisors

Professor Walid Magdy's group is where cross-platform social media analysis is done properly, including the multilingual dimension my project needs and my industrial datasets have always had; his work is the reason I know single-platform, English-only findings do not travel. When I wrote to him in March 2026 he replied that he was willing to be listed as a potential supervisor and told me to do my best to get a publication first. The publication now exists, and it is the foundation the doctorate extends. A natural Edinburgh-specific extension of Strand 1, which I would want to shape with him, is multilingual platform equivalence: whether the language facet and the platform facet interact, using Arabic and South Asian language communities where my collection experience is direct.

Dr Björn Ross replied to my first enquiry that the fit with his research was strong and pointed me to this CDT. His work on social bots and misinformation infrastructure connects to the project at a specific joint: the inauthenticity screen that decides what counts as public opinion before anything is measured, and the question of how coordinated and synthetic content distorts machine-labelled indices. Strand 2's live pipeline is also, operationally, the kind of system his social data science work interrogates.

Between them the two cover the project's axes, measurement across platforms and languages with Magdy, integrity of the measured population with Ross, and both have already seen and encouraged the direction. Edinburgh's ILCC more broadly, and the Alan Turing Institute connections, put the project inside the UK's strongest NLP environment.

## Feasibility, and what I bring the cohort

The usual risk in this design is the data engineering, and it is the risk I remove. I run a ten-platform daily ingestion pipeline and a 68-crawler fleet with drift alarms and CI gates in production; the collection layer of this project is a month-one capability, not a year-one gamble. The statistical protocol is published rather than promised. My own company's cascade supplies a standing industrial test case whose measured pathologies, 82.9 per cent inter-tier agreement on sentiment and 0.0 per cent on toxicity against 96.7 per cent self-reported confidence, are the problem in miniature. To the cohort I bring seven years of production practice, experience leading and mentoring engineers, a public technical guide practitioners actually use, and the habit of shipping. To the CDT's industrial-engagement agenda I bring live relationships with the social intelligence industry whose measurement practices this research would change, and a placement interest to match.

The plan fits the CDT's shape: the integrated first year sharpens the design and closes the gaps my industrial path left, particularly governance and co-creation methods; Strand 1 runs from year two on the UK spine already frozen in the foundation paper; the live pipeline for Strand 2 starts early because it needs elapsed time; Strand 3 reuses the same annotated spine rather than new collection; and the criterion test closes the thesis. A declared drop-order keeps the worst case inside four years. The result, if it works, is the thing the field is currently asking for in print and nobody owns: instruments that can be trusted, and shown to be trustworthy, which is what responsible NLP means when the NLP is doing the measuring.
