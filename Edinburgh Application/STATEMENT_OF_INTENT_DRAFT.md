# UKRI AI CDT in Responsible and Trustworthy NLP, University of Edinburgh, 2027 entry
# Rewrite in your own voice before submission. No em-dashes anywhere.

I have spent seven years building the systems this CDT exists to make responsible. At M+C Saatchi Fluency I operate a pipeline that ingests ten social platforms daily for enterprise clients including Amazon and Ford. At Dubizzle Labs I led the team running more than five hundred scrapers across fifteen countries. At ArtemisAI, the company I co-founded, 3.39 million comments have passed through a multi-model NLP cascade I designed. I am good at this work, and for most of those seven years I did not question what the numbers coming out of it actually meant.

The question caught up with me during my MSc at Portsmouth, which I completed with Distinction as a Chevening Scholar. My dissertation analysed 279,000 posts about UK economic policy across four platforms, with a fine-tuned BERT model reaching macro-F1 0.878 against BiLSTM and lexicon baselines. The finding that mattered was not the accuracy. It was that the same policy event produced structurally different sentiment profiles on different platforms, which meant the cross-platform aggregates my industry ships every day rest on an untested assumption: that the classifier measures the same thing everywhere it reads.

I went back to my own production systems and looked. In ArtemisAI's cascade, our two model tiers agree on 82.9 per cent of sentiment labels and 0.0 per cent of toxicity labels, while the toxicity model reports 96.7 per cent average confidence. Our dashboard's "accuracy" figure is, when you read the code, inter-model agreement with no ground truth behind it. Nobody built that dishonestly. It is simply what happens when a field adopts powerful instruments faster than it builds the discipline to check them, and it is why I stopped being satisfied with building the instruments and started wanting to work on their accountability.

That work has begun. With my MSc supervisor Dr Ella Haig I have written a methods paper, on arXiv as [ID / "posted August 2026"], that treats each social platform as a survey mode and specifies a six-step psychometric protocol for testing whether machine-labelled sentiment is measurement-equivalent across platforms. Its central result comes from a 500-replication simulation with known ground truth: a pooled index that takes platform scores at face value reports the wrong sign of aggregate sentiment on roughly a quarter of days, while a measurement-adjusted index does not. When Professor Magdy told me in March to do my best to get a publication before applying, this paper is what I did about it.

I want to do a PhD because the problem I care about has outgrown what I can do from inside industry. The field's instrument is now the LLM, which updates without notice, reads each platform differently, and carries a standpoint nobody quantifies, and the people relying on its outputs now include regulators and newsrooms, not just brands. Making that instrument accountable needs sustained research, not evenings around a product roadmap. My statement of alignment sets out the programme: calibration methods for LLM annotation across platforms, across model versions, and across the standpoints built into the models.

I am applying to this CDT specifically, and not only because Edinburgh is where Professor Magdy and Dr Ross already know my work and have said it fits. The CDT's framing of responsible NLP as something that must be designed, not audited after the fact, matches how I think an engineer should approach it. I have built data and models for seven years; the CDT's training in responsible data practices, explainability for real users, governance, and co-creation covers exactly the parts of the discipline my industrial career never taught me. And after seven years of being the only person in the room who worried about these questions, I want the cohort. I have led engineering teams and mentored juniors, I run a company, and I know the difference between working alone with a problem and working in a community that shares it.

I also bring something back. The usual risk in a data-heavy PhD is the collection and infrastructure; for me that part is a solved problem, with production fleets, drift monitoring and CI-gated pipelines as my day job, so my four years can be spent on the science. I maintain a public engineering guide on web data collection that practitioners use, which is my evidence that I finish public-facing work and that I can carry research standards back into industry. My aim after the doctorate is a research career at the boundary where I have lived my whole working life: making the measurement layer of NLP systems something regulators, researchers and companies can actually trust, through standards, audits and open tooling.

I am ready for this now in a way I was not three years ago. The question is nailed down, the first paper exists, both supervisors have seen the direction, and my industrial life keeps handing me new evidence that the problem is real. I would like to spend the next four years at Edinburgh turning that evidence into a discipline.
