## 1. Background and Motivation

Computational social science has changed its measurement instrument. Within three years, large language models moved from novelty to default annotator for social media text: they beat crowd workers on standard tasks at a twentieth of the cost (Gilardi et al. 2023), and the field adopted them. The evidence about the instrument itself is less comfortable. Reasonable configuration choices flip the statistical conclusions of published annotation studies about 31 per cent of the time (Baumann et al. 2025). Prompt wording alone moves label distributions by double digits (Camuffo et al. 2026). Proprietary models change behaviour under silent backend updates (Chen, Zaharia and Zou 2024), which is why methodologists warn against building research on APIs whose versions cannot be pinned (Ollion et al. 2024).

Between April and August 2026 the field converged on the diagnosis. Annotation is now argued to be a measurement act, not an agreement exercise (Harvey, Koenecke and Kizilcec 2026); annotation regimes, the pairing of model and prompt, are shown to produce different measurements of the same construct, with regime differences propagating into substantive results (Milkova et al. 2026); persona conditioning is shown to shift what models label as harmful along ideological lines (Civelli et al. 2025); and industry now has decision frameworks for whether a replacement model is safe to adopt (Casey et al. 2026). The field has diagnosed the problem. Nobody has built the calibration methods, and that is what I propose to do.

The problem is worst in **observatories**, which monitor for years and pool many platforms. The era of single-platform research ended for structural reasons; the 2023 closure of affordable Twitter/X access ended twenty years of growth in Twitter-based studies (Murtfeldt et al. 2024), and whole platforms now carry distinct, persistent ideological profiles, so apparent opinion shifts often reflect users sorting between platforms rather than changing their minds (Di Martino et al. 2026). Multi-platform designs are now the common case and the hard case: several platforms, read by LLM instruments, over years. An observatory running 2025 to 2030 will (a) read several platforms with one instrument without evidence it measures the same construct on each; (b) cross many silent model updates, so trend lines confound changes in the world with changes in the instrument; and (c) embed an epistemic standpoint in that instrument, since language models are not neutral readers (Nelimarkka 2026). Survey methodology solved the structural analogues decades ago: mode equivalence testing, bridging designs when instruments change mid-series (Kraemer et al. 2025), and documentation of the instrument's perspective.

I also see this problem from inside a production system. In my own production annotation systems (3.39 million comments through a multi-model cascade at ArtemisAI, the company I co-founded), measured inter-model agreement is 82.9 per cent for sentiment and 0.0 per cent for toxicity, while the toxicity model self-reports 96.7 per cent average confidence; the dashboard's operational "accuracy" figure is, on inspection of the code, inter-model agreement with no criterion behind it. The zero comes from mismatched label taxonomies and thresholds between the tiers, an equivalence failure, which is exactly what this project studies. This is the observatory problem in miniature, and I run it daily.

The foundation paper with Dr Ella Haig (Ikram and Haig 2026) supplies the starting machinery: a six-step psychometric protocol treating each platform as a survey mode, and a 500-replication known-truth simulation in which a naive pooled index reports the wrong sign of aggregate sentiment on roughly a quarter of days while a measurement-adjusted index does not. The doctoral project carries that machinery from fitted classifiers to the instruments the field now actually uses. The mode analogy has limits and the design respects them: survey modes reach one population through different channels, while platforms are different populations, which is why the selection layer and the measurement layer stay separate throughout.

## 2. Research Aims and Novel Contribution

**Aim.** To develop and empirically validate a calibration discipline for LLM-annotated public opinion measurement across platforms, over time, and across epistemic standpoints, so that longitudinal multi-platform monitoring can distinguish real opinion change from instrument change.

**Four contributions, each stated against its nearest neighbour:**

1. **Platform equivalence of LLM labels, formally tested.** Hou, Thapa and Tay (2026) built the equivalence framework with language as the axis and left platform open. The one platform-portable measure to date (Ameen and Healey 2026) validates by correlation with held-out external scores, which cannot detect scalar non-invariance, the failure mode that flips pooled trend signs in the foundation paper's simulation. The residual this project takes: fitted categorical invariance and DIF models with platform as the grouping variable and a human-coded criterion in the model.
2. **A bridging design for instrument change.** Existing work detects that an API model changed (Gao, Liang and Guestrin 2025), re-validates against fixed audit sets (Cheng, Mayya and Sedoc 2024), and decides when a replacement is safe to adopt (Casey et al. 2026). Nothing corrects the measurement series when versions are not equivalent. This project supplies the correction: a pinned open-weight shadow annotator as a fixed bridge anchor, stratified misclassification-matrix corrections, and a costed re-annotation policy, framed as the cost-bounded alternative to re-annotating an entire archive at every update.
3. **Standpoint as a measurement facet.** The supervisor's MarxistLLM (Nelimarkka 2026) builds standpoint-conditioned instruments and evaluates them qualitatively; persona work shows ideology shifts labels (Civelli et al. 2025) without decomposing the effect. Here, standpoint configurations enter a multivariate generalizability analysis whose reported objects are the covariance structure among standpoints and fixed-effect contrasts per construct, with platform crossings testing whether standpoint effects travel. The aim is to make the instrument's perspective visible and reportable rather than averaged away (Klein and D'Ignazio 2024).
4. **The criterion test: does calibration pay?** The state of the art in platform-based opinion estimation corrects selection and stops there, on a single platform (Cerina and Duch 2026). This project runs the missing horse race with the selection layer held identical across all index arms, so the comparison isolates the measurement contribution: naive pooled versus platform-specific versus calibrated indices, nowcasting probability-sample survey series.

No paper or group holds more than one of these cells; the observatory is what makes them one project. Statistical-correction methods fix estimates within one corpus and one instrument version (Gligorić et al. 2025; Egami et al. 2023); reliability scoring stops at reliability (Barrie, Palaiologou and Törnberg 2024); variance-aware protocols decompose pipeline facets without platform, time or standpoint (Camuffo et al. 2026).

## 3. Research Questions

1. **RQ1 (Equivalence).** Do LLM-scored opinion constructs achieve threshold and loading invariance across platforms discussing the same policy events, and where invariance fails, which items function differentially and by how much?
2. **RQ2 (Drift).** How large are the measurement discontinuities introduced by model updates in a running annotation pipeline, and can a shadow-anchored bridging design keep a multi-year opinion series comparable across versions at a stated fraction of full re-annotation cost?
3. **RQ3 (Standpoint).** How large and how stable are the effects of epistemic standpoint conditioning on LLM-scored opinion measurements, and do those effects travel across platforms?
4. **RQ4 (Criterion).** With selection correction held identical, does a measurement-calibrated multi-platform index nowcast survey-measured opinion better than a naive pooled index, and by how much?

## 4. Literature Context

**The instrument turn and its instability.** LLM annotation was legitimised by accuracy claims (Gilardi et al. 2023) and then complicated by what closer examination found: conclusion-flipping configuration sensitivity (Baumann et al. 2025), regime-dependent measurements (Milkova et al. 2026), and the recognition that annotation is measurement (Harvey, Koenecke and Kizilcec 2026). The field's remedies are one-shot criterion validation and statistical correction against gold labels; all of it treats the instrument as fixed and the corpus as one population.

**Time, drift and the observatory.** Model behaviour shifts under silent updates (Chen, Zaharia and Zou 2024); detection (Gao, Liang and Guestrin 2025), audit sets (Cheng, Mayya and Sedoc 2024) and migration decisions (Casey et al. 2026) exist. Media observatories are being built with serious infrastructure and no instrument-versioning methodology (Pehlivan et al. 2025). Survey research bridges instrument changes mid-series with parallel administration (Kraemer et al. 2025); that design has never been ported to AI annotation; there, the archive can be re-annotated, which changes the cost calculation but not the design logic.

**Standpoint and whose measurement.** Language models encode political and cultural positions (Santurkar et al. 2023), and the supervisor's programme has pushed this from complaint to instrument: MarxistLLM asks whether a model can analyse from a declared theoretical perspective (Nelimarkka 2026), and the group's earlier work showed different computational eyes see different things in the same images (Berg and Nelimarkka 2023). What is missing is the measurement bridge: treating standpoint as a designed facet and estimating its effects instead of assuming them.

**The supervisor's programme is the natural home.** The group's credo, the role of theory and the importance of validity and reliability in computational analysis, is this project's method; Platformed Interactions (Nelimarkka et al. 2020) is the in-group demonstration that platforms shape political communication; the Finnish Digital Observatory (2025-2030) is the running system this method is built to serve.

## 5. Methodology

**5.1 Design.** One **data spine**, four studies, four papers. The construct family is public attitudes toward government economic policy, chosen for three concrete reasons: it is the best survey-anchored construct family at monthly-or-better frequency in both countries; its anchor events (budgets, fiscal statements) are calendar-scheduled, giving pre-registrable windows no culture-war topic offers; and it extends my published measurement base rather than starting cold. The design is deliberately asymmetric: the **UK is the development arm** (the foundation paper's fifteen frozen fiscal events; weekly YouGov government-economic trackers as the rolling criterion, British Election Study waves for validation), and **Finland is the confirmatory arm** (anchor set co-designed with the supervisor around budjettiriihi and election moments; candidate criterion series named now: Statistics Finland's monthly consumer confidence indicators, EVA's attitude surveys, and Eurobarometer trust-in-government items; since none of these tracks government-policy attitudes at high frequency, the Finnish arm confirms Studies 1 to 3 while giving Study 4 low-frequency validation only, unless a monthly omnibus item is commissioned, which the funding plan prices as an option). Platforms: Reddit, YouTube and Bluesky, selected for currently credible research access, with a pre-registered density audit and a named substitution rule; for the Finnish arm the audit also weighs Suomi24, whose archives sit at Kielipankki on the same CSC infrastructure this project computes on, because Finnish-language volume on the main trio is thin, and a pilot pull publishes expected daily volumes per platform and language before any keel density is claimed. Two corpus layers: a low-volume **continuous keel** (a keyword and actor panel running the entire project, which is what Study 2's live pipeline annotates) and high-density **anchor windows** on top. The keel's sampling frame is versioned like the instrument, because frames decay too: scheduled embedding-based query expansion with logged versions, actor-cohort refresh with overlap periods, and a corpus-drift monitor (volume, vocabulary, author composition) running beside the model tripwire. Each anchor window is paired with a matched off-anchor reference window, since budget-day discourse is activated commentary as much as opinion; whether invariance holds on-anchor versus off-anchor is itself a pre-registered question, and the codebook separates news-link amplification from original commentary. Everything is archived under a provenance discipline: every raw item stored, every label logged with model identifier, endpoint, prompt hash, temperature, seed and timestamp, and a frozen probe set re-scored on schedule as the drift tripwire. Deletion is handled as a censoring process rather than an inconvenience: per-wave deletion rates are reported, hashes and label distributions of removed items are retained so attrition bias stays quantifiable, bridging estimates carry an attrition sensitivity analysis, and the Instrument Change Log records corpus deletions alongside model versions.

**5.2 Data and access.** Collection uses my own production tooling, and this is most of the feasibility argument: I currently operate, for enterprise clients including Amazon and Ford, a pipeline ingesting ten platforms daily into a governed 156-column master table and a 68-crawler fleet across 62 domains that collected 6.7 million items in six weeks under CI quality gates, KL-divergence drift alarms and operator-approved deletion controls. That commercial record is engineering pedigree, not a data source: academic collection here uses sanctioned APIs and university infrastructure only, under university governance. YouTube access is pursued via the researcher programme, and on the EU side via DSA Article 40 vetted-researcher status, for which a University of Helsinki affiliation qualifies; Article 40 does not reach the UK, so the UK arm rests on the researcher programme with the substitution rule as its fallback. Reddit access runs through Reddit for Researchers, whose terms are revocable and restrictive, which the risk register reflects; Bluesky through the AT Protocol firehose with archived captures. The human criterion: a stratified sample of 1,000 to 1,500 items per country, double-coded with 25 to 30 per cent overlap under a pre-registered multi-item codebook; Finnish annotation by native-speaker coders recruited through the group, with the codebook translated and back-translated, which is one of the concrete reasons this project belongs in Helsinki. The pre-registered selection layer includes an inauthenticity screen (account-age and activity heuristics, coordination detection on the keel, and a synthetic-text flag), and Study 4 reports every index with and without it; by 2026 part of the comment stream is itself machine-written, and the design must not score LLM output as public opinion.

**5.3 Studies.**
- **Study 1, Equivalence (UK arm from month 5, Finnish arm from month 9; through month 18).** The measurement model, stated: the unit is the post or comment, annotated with its parent context where threading matters; the construct is scored by the LLM on a pre-registered multi-item codebook (policy stance, intensity, blame attribution), so the items are codebook sub-constructs and the LLM is the scorer, not the item. Comments cluster in threads and a few heavy posters produce many items, so the models carry user- and thread-level clustering (multilevel CFA or cluster-robust corrections) and the Study 4 indices cap per-user contributions, weighting people rather than posts. Further, the human criterion sample enters the factor model as a second method, so shared machine misreading is detectable rather than invisible. Estimation: ordered-categorical CFA under WLSMV with theta parameterisation; the invariance sequence follows Wu and Estabrook (2016), thresholds before loadings, judged on dMACS-class effect sizes rather than fit indices alone; DIF with iterative anchor purification, alignment as the identification check. The platform-nesting confound is addressed with a common-item linking bank whose backbone is propensity-matched pairs (topic, length, register), because organically cross-posted opinion text is rare and skews toward spam and copypasta; transplantation experiments, identical texts scored under different platform-context prompts, isolate the scorer's platform-cue effect specifically, and organic cross-posts are kept only as a descriptive check. Two model families and a pre-registered prompt battery throughout, with language treated as an explicit facet rather than a nuisance: the battery includes Finnish-capable open-weight models, and a translated-item equivalence check runs before any cross-country claim is pooled. Repeated draws at fixed settings estimate the stochastic floor first, so DIF is not fitted to sampling noise.
- **Study 2, Drift (months 12-30).** Two regimes, designed so that neither depends on vendor timing. The backbone is a **pinned open-weight version ladder**: at least six planned version transitions that I execute as designed interventions with full parallel-run overlap on the keel. Vendor-forced and silent proprietary updates are opportunistic replications, handled by the second mechanism: a **continuously running pinned open-weight shadow annotator** on a rolling stratified reference sample, which serves as a fixed bridge anchor that no vendor can retire, plus retrospective re-annotation of stratified archive samples by the post-update model once the frozen-probe tripwire fires. The correction estimator is a stratified misclassification-matrix adjustment within pre-registered content strata (hundreds of items per stratum per event), with the stationarity assumption stated and tested; the whole design is the cost-bounded alternative to re-annotating the archive at every update, with the cost curve computed from real numbers (re-annotating a 6.7-million-item archive per update versus roughly one to two million calls per bridging event). This extends monitoring I already run in production, where weekly KL-divergence alarms watch pipeline output distributions.
- **Study 3, Standpoint (months 18-34, jointly with the supervisor).** Zero new collection: the frozen spine is re-annotated under standpoint conditions (baseline instruction, declared-perspective variants in the MarxistLLM tradition, and a formal-neutral control), crossed with model family and prompt on a fractional design, full crossing on one flagship platform-country pair. Analysis is a **multivariate generalizability study** in the Brennan (2001) tradition: standpoint is a fixed facet, so the reported objects are the covariance structure among standpoint-conditioned scores and fixed-effect contrasts per construct, a mixed-model decomposition rather than a random-effects generalisation claim; the declared residual is run-to-run variance at fixed settings. Platform crossings test whether standpoint effects travel, which is the invariance question at the second level.
- **Study 4, Criterion (months 26-42).** Naive pooled, platform-specific, and calibrated indices, with the **selection-adjustment layer held identical across all arms** in the Cerina and Duch tradition, so only the measurement layer varies; rolling-window nowcasts against the weekly UK tracker as the powered comparison and the Finnish series as replication; human panels treated as one more method under MTMM logic (Cernat et al. 2025), with the survey criterion as the external terminus. A null result, calibration does not pay, is itself publishable in the post-Gayo-Avello literature.

**5.4 Validation, robustness and staging.** Effect-size criteria and sensitivity analyses across model families and prompts throughout; open-weight models pinned for reproducibility, proprietary models handled through the Study 2 machinery rather than trust. Declared drop-order if time compresses: Study 3 folds into Study 1 as an additional facet; Study 4's design is locked at month 26 because the adjacent literature is moving. Months 43-48 are reserved for thesis compilation, revisions and defence; that buffer is a large part of why four years is enough.

## 7. Expected Contributions

**Academic.** Fitted equivalence evidence for LLM-scored opinion with platform as the group, which nobody has produced; a bridging design that corrects a series instead of only deciding whether to migrate; a variance decomposition of the standpoint inside the instrument; and the calibrated-versus-naive comparison the nowcasting literature has so far skipped.

## 9. Ethics and Risk Management

Public content only. Political-opinion texts are special-category data under GDPR Article 9; the legal basis chain is Article 9(2)(j) scientific-research processing under Article 89 safeguards as implemented by the Finnish Data Protection Act (1050/2018) research derogations, with a data protection impact assessment in Year 1, building on my ethics-approved MSc procedures (Portsmouth TETHIC-2025-111094). ## 11. Conclusion

The field now measures opinion with instruments that update without notice, behave differently across platforms, and carry a standpoint nobody has quantified, and it relies on them most in long-running multi-platform monitoring. The 2026 literature states the problem clearly; what it lacks is the discipline survey methodology built for the same situation two generations ago. This project ports that discipline, with the identification details spelled out. I bring a published psychometric protocol, six years of production platform-data engineering, and daily contact with the problem, from a ten-platform pipeline to a multi-model cascade I built and cannot yet fully defend. The Helsinki Social Computing Group is the one place where the measurement machinery, the standpoint theory and the running observatory already sit in the same room.

### Summary (cap 1,000) - 996 characters

Computational social science now measures public opinion by having large language models read social media, pooled across platforms over years. Three properties of this instrument are untested: whether its scores mean the same thing on every platform, whether they stay comparable when the model is updated, and how much they depend on the standpoint built into the model. This project develops and validates the missing calibration discipline. Four studies share one data spine (UK and Finnish economic-policy discourse, three platforms, survey anchor series): fitted measurement-invariance and DIF tests with platform as the group; a shadow-anchored bridging design that keeps a series comparable across model updates; a multivariate generalizability analysis of epistemic standpoint; and a criterion test of whether a calibrated multi-platform index nowcasts surveys better than naive pooling. Deliverables: an open toolkit and reporting standard, piloted with the Finnish Digital Observatory.

### Theoretical rationale (cap 3,000) - 2477 characters

Large language models became the default annotator for social media text within three years, and the evidence about the instrument arrived just as fast: configuration choices flip published conclusions about 31 per cent of the time (Baumann et al. 2025), prompt wording moves label distributions by double digits (Camuffo et al. 2026), and proprietary models change behaviour under silent updates (Chen, Zaharia and Zou 2024). Work from April to August 2026 makes the case plainly: annotation is a measurement act, not an agreement exercise (Harvey, Koenecke and Kizilcec 2026); model-prompt regimes produce different measurements whose differences propagate into findings (Milkova et al. 2026); persona conditioning shifts labels along ideological lines (Civelli et al. 2025); industry has frameworks for deciding when a replacement model is safe (Casey et al. 2026). The field has the diagnosis but not the calibration methods.

The need concentrates in observatories. Multi-platform pooling became the only viable design after the 2023 API closures (Murtfeldt et al. 2024), while platforms carry distinct, persistent ideological profiles (Di Martino et al. 2026). A monitoring system running 2025 to 2030 therefore reads several platforms with one instrument that updates silently and embeds a standpoint (Nelimarkka 2026): its trend lines confound changes in the world with changes in the instrument. Survey methodology solved the structural analogues decades ago through mode-equivalence testing and bridging designs when instruments change mid-series (Kraemer et al. 2025). This project ports that discipline to LLM-annotated platform data.

The theoretical base is my foundation paper with Dr Ella Haig: platforms are survey modes, so the psychometric equivalence toolkit applies with platform as the group; a 500-replication known-truth simulation shows a naive pooled index reports the wrong sign of aggregate sentiment on a quarter of days while an adjusted index does not. I also run the problem daily: in my production systems, inter-model agreement is 82.9 per cent for sentiment and 0.0 per cent for toxicity while the toxicity model self-reports 96.7 per cent confidence, an equivalence failure between the tiers. The project sits squarely in the Social Computing Group's programme: theory plus validity and reliability as method, MarxistLLM extended quantitatively, and the Finnish Digital Observatory as the running system whose honesty the method underwrites.

### Objectives and expected impact (cap 1,500) - 1362 characters

Aim: to develop and empirically validate a calibration discipline for LLM-annotated public opinion measurement across platforms, over time, and across epistemic standpoints, so longitudinal multi-platform monitoring can distinguish real opinion change from instrument change.

Objectives. O1 (Equivalence): fit categorical measurement-invariance and DIF models to LLM-scored opinion constructs with platform as the grouping variable and a human-coded criterion in the model; nobody has fitted these models. O2 (Drift): quantify measurement discontinuities from model updates and validate a shadow-anchored bridging design that corrects the series, at a stated fraction of full re-annotation cost. O3 (Standpoint): estimate, in a multivariate generalizability design, how large and how stable standpoint-conditioning effects are, and whether they travel across platforms. O4 (Criterion): with selection correction held identical, test whether a calibrated multi-platform index nowcasts survey-measured opinion better than naive pooling.

Impact: the first calibration methodology for AI-instrumented opinion measurement; an open toolkit and a reporting standard adoptable by observatories, regulators exercising DSA data access, the survey profession, and industry; and operating methodology for the Finnish Digital Observatory across its 2025-2030 model updates.

### Methods and materials (cap 4,000) - 3677 characters

One data spine, four studies, four papers. Construct family: public attitudes toward government economic policy, chosen because it is the best survey-anchored construct family at monthly-or-better frequency in both countries, its anchor events are calendar-scheduled (pre-registrable windows), and it extends my published measurement base. Asymmetric arms: the UK is the development arm (fifteen frozen fiscal events published in the foundation paper; weekly YouGov trackers as rolling criterion; British Election Study waves for validation); Finland is the confirmatory arm (anchors co-designed with the supervisor; candidate criterion series: Statistics Finland consumer confidence, EVA surveys, Eurobarometer trust items). Platforms: Reddit, YouTube, Bluesky, under a pre-registered density audit with a named substitution rule; YouTube access additionally via DSA Article 40 vetted-researcher status. Two corpus layers: a continuous keel (fixed keyword and actor panel, a few thousand items per day, running the whole project) and high-density anchor windows. Human criterion: 1,000 to 1,500 items per country, double-coded with 25 to 30 per cent overlap; Finnish annotation by native-speaker coders recruited through the group.

Study 1, Equivalence (months 5-18). Unit: the post. The construct is scored on a pre-registered multi-item codebook (policy stance, intensity, blame attribution); the LLM is the scorer, not the item, and the human criterion enters the factor model as a second method so shared machine misreading is detectable. Ordered-categorical CFA under WLSMV, invariance sequence per Wu and Estabrook (2016), dMACS effect sizes, DIF with iterative anchor purification. The platform-nesting confound is broken by a common-item linking bank: cross-posted texts, matched pairs, and platform-context prompt manipulations on identical texts. Two model families, a pre-registered prompt battery, stochastic floor estimated first.

Study 2, Drift (months 12-30). Backbone: a pinned open-weight version ladder, at least six planned transitions executed as designed interventions with full parallel overlap on the keel. Silent and vendor-forced updates are handled by a continuously running pinned shadow annotator on a rolling stratified reference sample plus retrospective re-annotation once the frozen-probe tripwire fires. Correction: stratified misclassification-matrix adjustment within pre-registered content strata, stationarity stated and tested; framed as the cost-bounded alternative to full archive re-annotation.

Study 3, Standpoint (months 18-34, with the supervisor). Zero new collection: the frozen spine re-annotated under standpoint conditions (baseline, declared-perspective variants in the MarxistLLM tradition, formal-neutral control) on a fractional design. Analysis: multivariate generalizability study (Brennan 2001); reported objects are the covariance structure among standpoint-conditioned scores and fixed-effect contrasts; platform crossings test whether effects travel.

Study 4, Criterion (months 26-42). Naive pooled, platform-specific and calibrated indices with the selection-adjustment layer held identical across arms; rolling nowcasts against the weekly UK tracker, Finland as replication; human panels as one method under MTMM logic. A null result is publishable.

Comments nest in threads and heavy users repeat, so models carry user- and thread-level clustering and the indices cap per-user contributions. The linking bank's backbone is propensity-matched pairs; organic cross-posts are rare and kept only as a check. Drop-order if time compresses: Study 3 folds into Study 1 as a facet; Study 4's design locks at month 26.

### Data plan (cap 1,000) - 966 characters

Public platform content only, collected via official and researcher APIs into university and CSC storage. Every raw item is archived; every label carries a provenance record (model identifier, endpoint, prompt hash, temperature, seed, timestamp), and a frozen probe set is re-scored on schedule as the drift tripwire; this archive is what makes retrospective bridging possible. Retention follows a documented schedule with platform deletion-propagation compliance, a control I already operate in production. Identifiers are hashed at ingestion; quotations are paraphrased in outputs. Derived aggregates, codebooks, model configurations and analysis code are published openly with each paper; raw text is shared only under the platforms' terms and GDPR safeguards. Deletion is logged as a censoring process, with per-wave rates reported. Data management follows University of Helsinki and Finnish Social Science Data Archive practice, with a DMP finalised in month 3.

### Ethics (cap 1,000) - 859 characters

Political-opinion texts are special-category data under GDPR Article 9. The legal basis chain is Article 9(2)(j) scientific-research processing under Article 89 safeguards as implemented by the Finnish Data Protection Act (1050/2018) research derogations, with a data protection impact assessment in Year 1, building on my ethics-approved MSc procedures (University of Portsmouth, TETHIC-2025-111094) re-approved under University of Helsinki review before collection. No private or restricted content; hashed identifiers; aggregate reporting; paraphrased quotations so posts are not re-identifiable by search; annotators paid and briefed, with wellbeing safeguards for exposure to hostile content. Platform terms of service govern collection modes per platform, and the density audit absorbs the resulting asymmetries by design rather than circumventing them.
