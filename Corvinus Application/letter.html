Your group studies how platforms and AI change where and how skilled software work is done, and I would like to spend the next years measuring that change rather than only living it.

Your work. The paper I keep returning to is Who is using AI to code? with Daniotti, Feng and Neffke (Science, 2026). A classifier for AI-written Python, applied to more than 30 million GitHub commits, estimates that AI wrote about 29% of US Python functions by the end of 2024 and raised output by about 3.6%, with the gains going to experienced developers while early-career developers, the keenest users, showed no significant benefit. That fits what I see: an agent saves me time only because years of writing such code by hand let me judge its output quickly. The paper is open about its limits, open-source Python on GitHub in six countries, and asks for countries at different income levels. Your map of Claude Code on GitHub shows a related limit. It counts commits carrying Claude Code's default co-author trailer. My public commits carry it, but my profile gives no location and most of the agent-written code I ship goes into private client repositories, so a developer like me is largely invisible to it. With del Rio-Chanona and Laurentsyeva (PNAS Nexus, 2024) you showed that Stack Overflow activity fell by about 25% within six months of ChatGPT, relative to sites where it was less available or less capable: the public traces we study are themselves changed by the technology under study. And The Geography of Open Source Software (2022) and Digital Traces of Brain Drain (2023) show how far GitHub locations can go in tracking where developers are and where they move.

A question I could contribute to. How do AI coding agents change the division of labour in software work, and for whom? Agents now leave signatures in public traces: co-author trailers, bot accounts that open pull requests, and the code patterns your classifier detects. Following developers' commits and collaboration networks before and after they start working with agents, one could ask whether agent use changes who leads a project and the uneven workloads that your paper with Betti, Gallo and Battiston (Nature Communications, 2025) links to success, whether review work shifts towards senior developers, and whether developers in lower-income countries, including the many who, like me, enter global software production through freelance platforms and remote contracts, see the gains your Science paper finds. I would bring one habit from my own research: asking how much of a measured change comes from the platform's visibility rules rather than from behaviour. A trailer that is on by default for one tool and absent for another, a blank profile location, or work moving into private repositories can each look like a change in adoption. I would build that into the design, for example by checking trace-based measures against repositories where the true agent use is known, starting with my own.

My dissertation analysed public reaction to UK economic policies in about 279,000 posts from four platforms, with a fine-tuned BERT classifier against baselines, McNemar and bootstrap tests and Prophet forecasting. That led to a manuscript with my supervisor, Dr Ella Haig, Is the Platform Part of the Measurement?, complete and not yet published, which treats each platform as a survey mode.
