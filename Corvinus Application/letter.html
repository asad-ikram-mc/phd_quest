Your work. The paper I keep returning to is Who is using AI to code? with Daniotti, Feng and Neffke (Science, 2026). It estimates that AI wrote about 29% of US developers' Python functions by the end of 2024 and raised their output by about 3.6%, and that the gains went to experienced developers, not to the early-career developers who used AI more. That matches what I see: an agent saves me time only because years of writing such code by hand let me judge its output quickly. The paper is open about its limits, open-source Python on GitHub in six countries, and calls for widening the analysis to countries at different income levels. Pakistan is outside that sample, and so is much of the work developers like me do, in freelance orders and private client repositories. Your paper with del Rio-Chanona and Laurentsyeva (PNAS Nexus, 2024) adds a caution I take seriously: Stack Overflow activity fell by about 25% after ChatGPT's release, relative to sites where it was less available, so the public traces we study are themselves changed by the technology under study. Together they point to the problem I most want to work on: measuring a change in work when that change also alters what the data can see.

Building AI, and checking it. Finding that our own numbers measured the wrong thing is the most useful research lesson I have had.

A question I could contribute to. How do AI coding agents change the division of labour in software work, and for whom? Agents now leave signatures in public traces: co-author trailers in commits, bot accounts that open pull requests, and the code patterns your classifier detects. Following developers' commits, collaboration networks and, through LinkedIn, their careers before and after they start working with agents, one could ask whether agent use changes who leads a project and the uneven workloads that your paper with Betti, Gallo and Battiston (Nature Communications, 2025) links to success; whether review work shifts towards senior developers; and whether developers in lower-income countries, including the many who enter global software work through freelance platforms, see the gains your Science paper finds. I would build in the habit my own research gave me: asking how much of a measured change comes from a platform's visibility rules rather than from behaviour. A trailer that is on by default for one tool and absent for another, a blank profile location, or work moving into private repositories can each look like a change in adoption. I would check trace-based measures against repositories where true agent use is known, starting with my own.
